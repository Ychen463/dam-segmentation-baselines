\end{highlights}

\begin{abstract}
Heterogeneous dual-teacher knowledge distillation (DTKD)---combining a task-specific teacher with a foundation-model teacher---has shown promising gains on patch-level infrastructure datasets, but how robust are these gains to evaluation protocol changes?
We investigate this question on the \textbf{DamSegment} dataset \citep{damsegment2026} (1{,}500 images, 3~classes) using a SegFormer-B2 student with a parallel crack-enhancement branch.
On the original random split, multi-seed matched controls (5 conditions $\times$ 3 seeds) confirm that DTKD adds +1.5~mIoU$_{\text{fg}}$ with 5--7$\times$ variance reduction.
However, when all models are retrained on a leakage-free balanced group split (135 feature clusters, group-disjoint partitioning), the student (76.7\%) no longer surpasses its best teacher (78.6\%), reversing the method ranking.
A pixel-level oracle analysis on the validation set reveals +8.2~mIoU$_{\text{fg}}$ complementarity between the two teachers, confirming that the knowledge exists but cannot be automatically extracted: a selective distillation strategy that routes Teacher~2's knowledge based on pixel-level confidence fails to transfer the complementarity and exhibits a val--test ranking inversion.
Two further post-hoc interventions---continued ground-truth fine-tuning and group-aware resampling, neither of which involves Teacher~2---also fail to improve the strong Teacher~1 initialisation.
A parameter-matched branch comparison (DSConv vs.\ DCNv2 vs.\ standard convolution, 3 seeds each) additionally finds no measurable benefit from geometric inductive bias.
These results provide concrete evidence that (i)~evaluation protocol can reverse method rankings on patch-level datasets, (ii)~oracle complementarity does not guarantee extractable distillation gains when routing relies on pixel-level confidence, and (iii)~a strong task-specific initialisation resists further improvement on small datasets.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Concrete dams are safety-critical infrastructure whose structural integrity must be continuously monitored throughout multi-decade service lives.
Surface defects---predominantly cracks and spalling---serve as early indicators of internal deterioration, yet their manual inspection remains labour-intensive, subjective, and logistically constrained \citep{chen2024damsurvey,li2024dam}.
Automated semantic segmentation of these defects from high-resolution imagery therefore promises substantial gains in inspection efficiency and repeatability.

Recent deep-learning methods have advanced pixel-level defect segmentation considerably.
Encoder--decoder architectures such as U-Net \citep{ronneberger2015unet} and hierarchical transformers like SegFormer \citep{xie2021segformer} achieve competitive IoU on benchmark crack datasets \citep{zou2019deepcrack,liu2019deepcrack}.
However, IoU is insensitive to topological errors: a prediction that fragments a continuous crack may still score favourably while misrepresenting the structural extent of the defect \citep{pantoja2022topoloss}.
For structural assessment, crack \emph{connectivity} is as important as pixel coverage.

Topology-preserving losses such as clDice \citep{shit2021cldice} and persistent-homology penalties \citep{hu2019topology} partially address this gap, and the Skeleton Recall Loss (SRL) \citep{kirchhoff2024skeleton} reduces computational overhead substantially.
Meanwhile, foundation models such as SAM \citep{kirillov2023sam} offer powerful pretrained representations, yet in our experiments, LoRA fine-tuning \citep{hu2022lora} on small industrial datasets underperforms fully fine-tuned task-specific architectures.
A natural question arises: \emph{can the complementary strengths of task-specific and foundation models be combined}?

We investigate this through knowledge distillation, constructing a dual-teacher knowledge distillation (DTKD) framework and subjecting it to progressively stricter evaluation protocols.
We evaluate on the DamSegment dataset \citep{damsegment2026}, comprising 1{,}500 annotated dam surface images.
Our contributions are empirical findings rather than method proposals:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \emph{Evaluation-protocol sensitivity of DTKD}: heterogeneous dual-teacher KD combining a task-specific teacher with a foundation-model teacher adds +1.5~mIoU$_{\text{fg}}$ on the original random split (multi-seed), but the student no longer surpasses Teacher~1 on a leakage-free balanced group split, reversing the method ranking (Sections~\ref{sec:ablation},~\ref{sec:balanced_split}).
    \item \emph{Oracle complementarity vs.\ extractable gains}: pixel-level oracle analysis reveals +8.2~mIoU$_{\text{fg}}$ complementarity between teachers, but selective distillation based on pixel-level confidence fails to transfer it; two further post-hoc interventions (continued GT fine-tuning, group-aware resampling) also fail to improve the strong Teacher~1 initialisation (Section~\ref{sec:rescue}).
    \item A \emph{negative result on geometric inductive bias}: parameter-matched comparison of Dynamic Snake Convolution, DCNv2, and standard convolution (3 seeds each, ${\sim}$1.32M parameters) shows no measurable difference (Section~\ref{sec:ablation}).
    \item \emph{Failure mechanism analysis}: we identify three root causes---evaluation-protocol artefacts in patch-level datasets, the inability to route complementary knowledge via pixel-level confidence, and resistance of a strong initialisation to further optimisation on small datasets---providing practical guidance for future distillation research on small infrastructure datasets (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Concrete defect segmentation}

Early CNN-based crack detectors such as CrackNet \citep{zhang2017cracknet} treated defect identification as pixel-level classification.
Fully convolutional designs subsequently enabled dense prediction: DeepCrack \citep{liu2019deepcrack} introduced deeply-supervised side outputs, while \citet{zou2019deepcrack} exploited hierarchical multi-scale fusion.
Transformer-based architectures have demonstrated improved global context modelling: CrackFormer \citep{liu2021crackformer} embeds self-attention for long-range crack continuity, and SegFormer \citep{xie2021segformer} provides a lightweight hierarchical encoder.
Applied to dam inspection, \citet{arafin2024concrete} performed multi-class concrete defect segmentation using DeepLabV3+, \citet{li2024dam} proposed a lightweight CNN-Transformer hybrid, and CCT~Net \citep{ling2025cctnet} combined CNN and Transformer branches for dam crack segmentation.

\subsection{Topology-aware segmentation}

\citet{hu2019topology} introduced a differentiable topological loss based on persistent homology, and \citet{clough2020topological} extended it to cardiac imaging.
For tubular structures, clDice \citep{shit2021cldice} proposed a differentiable soft-skeleton metric.
The Skeleton Recall Loss \citep{kirchhoff2024skeleton} pre-computes ground-truth skeletons on CPU with over 90\% memory reduction.
Recent work has critically examined topology-aware losses \citep{arora2025srlcritique,joo2026cts}.

\subsection{Deformable and dynamic convolutions}

Deformable Convolutional Networks (DCN) \citep{dai2017deformable} introduced learnable offsets for adaptive sampling.
Dynamic Snake Convolution (DSConv) \citep{qi2023dynamic} constrains offsets along a one-dimensional path for elongated structures.
\citet{yu2024dscformer} proposed a dual-branch architecture combining DSConv with SegFormer for road crack segmentation.
Our work addresses multi-class segmentation, investigates DTKD for complementary knowledge transfer, and provides parameter-matched controls showing that DSConv's geometric bias provides no measurable benefit.

\subsection{Knowledge distillation for segmentation}

Knowledge distillation \citep{hinton2015distilling} transfers knowledge from teacher to student by matching softened distributions.
Multi-teacher distillation \citep{you2017learning} combines multiple specialised teachers.
\citet{zhang2022camkd} proposed confidence-aware multi-teacher KD at the sample level.
\citet{liang2025taskspecifickd} demonstrated task-specific KD from vision foundation models for medical segmentation.
Our study combines a task-specific teacher with a foundation-model teacher and critically examines whether their complementary strengths can be reliably extracted through distillation.

\subsection{Foundation models for segmentation}

SAM \citep{kirillov2023sam} and DINOv2 \citep{oquab2024dinov2} offer powerful vision foundation models.
LoRA \citep{hu2022lora} enables parameter-efficient adaptation.
\citet{ye2024samdefect} applied SAM for structural damage detection.
In our experiments, using foundation models as \emph{teachers} in knowledge distillation outperforms direct LoRA fine-tuning, though the comparison is limited by the absence of systematic hyperparameter tuning for LoRA baselines.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Overview}

Fig.~\ref{fig:architecture} illustrates the studied framework. The architecture enhances a SegFormer-B2 backbone at two levels: (1)~\emph{feature extraction} via a parallel branch, and (2)~\emph{knowledge transfer} via dual-teacher distillation (DTKD).

\begin{figure*}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.92\textwidth]{figures/fig_architecture.png}
    \caption{Overview of the studied DTKD framework. (a)~A SegFormer-B2 encoder with a parallel DSConv branch produces 3-class segmentation logits; the branch additively enhances only the crack channel. (b)~Dual-teacher knowledge distillation (DTKD): Teacher~1 (DSConv+SRL, strong on pixel accuracy) and Teacher~2 (SAM-LoRA+SRL, strong on spalling component recall) are combined by equal-weight logit averaging.}
    \label{fig:architecture}
\end{figure*}

\subsection{SegFormer-B2 backbone}

We adopt SegFormer-B2 \citep{xie2021segformer} as our backbone, providing a hierarchical Mix Transformer (MiT) encoder with four stages at resolutions $H/4$, $H/8$, $H/16$, and $H/32$ with channel dimensions 64, 128, 320, and 512. The all-MLP decoder fuses multi-scale features via linear projection and upsampling. We initialise from weights pretrained on ADE20K at $512{\times}512$.

\subsection{Parallel crack-enhancement branch}
\label{sec:dsconv}

We add a lightweight parallel branch (1.32M parameters, +4.8\% over SegFormer-B2) that produces a single-channel crack enhancement signal:
\begin{equation}
    \tilde{\mathbf{z}}_{\text{crack}} = \mathbf{z}_{\text{crack}} + \uparrow\!\mathbf{E}_{\text{crack}}
    \label{eq:dsconv}
\end{equation}
The branch fuses encoder stages~2 and~3 (at $H/8$ and $H/16$) via $1{\times}1$ projections to a hidden dimension $d{=}64$, followed by two parallel processing paths (one per spatial axis) and a two-layer head (Conv2d $64{\to}32{\to}1$, zero-initialised so the branch starts as a no-op).

\paragraph{Branch type does not matter}
Parameter-matched controls (Section~\ref{sec:ablation}, Table~\ref{tab:branch_compare}) comparing DSConv, DCNv2 \citep{dai2017deformable}, and standard convolution---all with ${\sim}$1.32M parameters and 3 seeds each---show no measurable difference across any metric.
We retain DSConv in the default configuration but do not claim the branch type as a contribution.

\subsection{Dual-teacher knowledge distillation}
\label{sec:dtkd}

Different model architectures excel at different quality dimensions: the task-specific DSConv+SRL model (Teacher~1) achieves higher pixel-level accuracy (IoU), while SAM-LoRA (Teacher~2) achieves higher component recall (CompR$_{\text{fg}}$), driven by substantially stronger spalling component preservation (CompR$_{\text{sp}}$ 86.0\% vs.\ 73.4\%; Table~\ref{tab:foundation}).

\paragraph{Teacher models} We use two pretrained teachers, both trained with SRL \citep{kirchhoff2024skeleton}:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item \textbf{Teacher~1} ($T_1$): DSConv+SRL (task-specific, strong on pixel accuracy)
    \item \textbf{Teacher~2} ($T_2$): SAM-LoRA+SRL (foundation model, strong on spalling component recall)
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Teacher ensemble}
The two teacher predictions are combined by equal-weight logit averaging:
\begin{equation}
    \tilde{\mathbf{z}} = \tfrac{1}{2}\,(\mathbf{z}_{T_1} / \tau + \mathbf{z}_{T_2} / \tau), \qquad
    \tilde{\mathbf{p}} = \text{softmax}(\tilde{\mathbf{z}})
    \label{eq:dtkd_ensemble}
\end{equation}
This construction is parameter-free and treats both teachers symmetrically. A per-class probability-space variant yields near-identical results (Supplementary~S2).

\paragraph{Distillation loss} The student is trained with:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{KD}} = \tau^2 \cdot \text{KL}\!\left(\tilde{\mathbf{p}} \,\|\, \text{softmax}(\mathbf{z}_s / \tau)\right)
    \label{eq:kd_loss}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Total loss} The training objective balances segmentation and distillation:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L} = (1-\alpha) \left[\lambda_1 \mathcal{L}_{\text{CE}} + \lambda_2 \mathcal{L}_{\text{Dice}}\right] + \alpha \, \mathcal{L}_{\text{KD}}
    \label{eq:total_loss}
\end{equation}
with $\alpha = 0.5$, $\tau = 4$, and $\lambda_1 = \lambda_2 = 0.5$.
SRL is used only during \emph{teacher} training; the student does not use SRL, as it conflicts with the distillation objective (Section~\ref{sec:ablation}).
An agreement-aware variant that modulates KD loss by pixel-level teacher agreement is explored in Supplementary~S1.

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Dataset}

We evaluate on the \textbf{semantic segmentation subset} of DamSegment \citep{damsegment2026}, which contains 1{,}500 pixel-level annotated images with three classes (background, crack, spalling) at $640{\times}640$ original resolution, organised into three difficulty tiers (Easy, Medium, Hard; 500 each).
All images are resized to $512{\times}512$ for training and evaluation.
We split the data into train/val/test sets of 1{,}200/150/150 images, stratified by tier.
Fig.~\ref{fig:dataset} shows representative samples; crack pixels range from 0.3\% to 7.9\% and spalling from 1.0\% to 11.7\%.

\subsection{Evaluation metrics}

We report complementary metrics:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item \textbf{mIoU$_{\text{fg}}$}: Mean IoU of crack and spalling classes.
    \item \textbf{IoU$_{\text{cr}}$}, \textbf{IoU$_{\text{sp}}$}: Per-class IoU.
    \item \textbf{BF1}: Boundary F1 score (2-pixel tolerance) \citep{csurka2013good}.
    \item \textbf{clDice}: Centreline Dice via skeletonisation \citep{shit2021cldice}.
    \item \textbf{CompR}: Component Recall---fraction of ground-truth connected components for which $\geq$50\% of pixels are correctly predicted. Additional component-level metrics (CompP, CompF1, PathCont) are defined in Supplementary~S6.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Single- vs.\ dual-teacher KD}
Neither single teacher matches the dual-teacher result: T1-only achieves 69.0\% and T2-only 69.2\%, both below the no-KD baseline (70.4\%) and well below DTKD (72.3\%).
This indicates that a single teacher's soft labels can introduce more noise than useful information when the teacher lacks complementary knowledge.

\paragraph{(b)$\to$(c): Adding DTKD}
DTKD provides substantial improvement: mIoU$_{\text{fg}}$ +1.9, IoU$_{\text{sp}}$ +2.8, clDice$_{\text{cr}}$ +1.5, and CompR$_{\text{fg}}$ +2.6.

\paragraph{Effect of SRL (rows e--f)}
\label{sec:srl}
SRL \citep{kirchhoff2024skeleton} penalises low predicted probability at ground-truth skeleton pixels:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{SRL}} = 1 - \frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}|} \sum_{s \in \mathcal{S}} p_{\text{crack}}(s)
    \label{eq:srl}
\end{equation}
Adding SRL to student training under DTKD (row f) reduces performance compared to the SRL-free pipeline (row c): mIoU$_{\text{fg}}$ 71.2\% vs.\ 72.3\% ($-$1.1), suggesting an optimisation conflict between SRL and KD objectives. Multi-seed experiments confirm that the SRL path is unstable (std 2.42 vs.\ 0.52 for DTKD, Table~\ref{tab:multiseed}).

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Why does evaluation protocol reverse the ranking?}
On the original random split, DTKD adds +1.5~mIoU$_{\text{fg}}$ (multi-seed); on the balanced group split, the student falls 1.9 points below Teacher~1 (Section~\ref{sec:balanced_split}).
DamSegment comprises 1{,}500 patches from a single dam site, and the original random split allows spatially adjacent patches to appear in both training and test sets.
Feature-cluster analysis reveals that 135 visual groups exhibit high intra-group similarity (cosine distance $<$0.3); when the split ensures group-disjoint partitioning, the model must generalise across visual modes rather than interpolate within them.
DTKD's variance reduction (5--7$\times$) on the original split likely reflects soft-label smoothing of patch-level leakage rather than genuine complementary knowledge transfer.
This finding has practical implications: \emph{patch-level datasets for infrastructure inspection should be split by visual groups, not randomly}.

\paragraph{Oracle complementarity: real but inaccessible}
The +8.2~mIoU$_{\text{fg}}$ pixel-level oracle (Section~\ref{sec:rescue}) is not a trivial upper bound---it confirms that Teacher~2 (SAM-LoRA) genuinely complements Teacher~1 on specific pixel regions, particularly for spalling boundaries.
However, the only strategy that actively attempts to transfer this complementarity---confidence-based selective KD (CKD)---fails because max-probability does not correlate with geometric quality (a teacher can be confident and topologically wrong).
This gap between oracle potential and achievable gains may be a general phenomenon in heterogeneous distillation: when teachers differ qualitatively (pixel accuracy vs.\ component recall) rather than quantitatively, simple routing metrics are insufficient.

\paragraph{Continued fine-tuning degradation}
Group-aware fine-tuning experiments (G0, G0R, G2) all showed monotonic validation degradation from the Teacher~1 initialisation, with the best epoch consistently at epoch~1.
C0 (GT-only fine-tuning) produced a negligible validation improvement (+0.04 at best epoch~15) and an exploratory test gain (+0.88) that cannot be treated as confirmatory given single-seed evaluation and test-set exposure during development.
Overall, this pattern---a strong pretrained model that resists meaningful improvement by further training on the same data---suggests either (i)~the 1{,}202-image training set provides insufficient diversity for continued learning, or (ii)~the Teacher~1 checkpoint occupies a sharp minimum that is easily disrupted.
This has practical implications for transfer learning in small-dataset regimes: \emph{fine-tuning from a strong task-specific checkpoint is not always beneficial}.

\paragraph{DTKD: benefits and limitations}
On the original split, DTKD's $2{\times}2$ factorial reveals that benefits require the parallel branch (architectural affinity with Teacher~1).
DTKD's hyperparameters ($\tau$, $\alpha$) are robust: excluding $\tau{=}2$, all settings fall within 0.9\% of the default (Supplementary~S4).
However, these properties are established only on the original split and may not transfer to stricter evaluation protocols.

\paragraph{SRL: useful for teachers, harmful for students}
SRL-trained teachers produce better students (+1.2~mIoU$_{\text{fg}}$) despite SRL degrading the teacher's own component coverage. As a student training component, SRL interferes with soft-label supervision ($-$1.1~mIoU$_{\text{fg}}$, single run). This asymmetry---that optimal teacher and student recipes differ---has broader implications for distillation pipeline design.

\paragraph{Branch geometry: a null result}
Parameter-matched controls (Table~\ref{tab:branch_compare}) show that DSConv's snake-shaped sampling provides no measurable advantage over DCNv2 or standard convolution across all segmentation and topology metrics.
The parallel branch provides useful capacity, but its geometric inductive bias does not matter on this dataset.

\paragraph{Limitations}
The model underperforms on thin cracks (mean width ${\sim}$6~px at $512{\times}512$ training resolution in the Hard tier, with some regions as narrow as ${\sim}$3~px) and co-occurring crack--spalling regions.
All experiments use a single dataset from one dam site; the balanced group split mitigates within-site leakage but does not address cross-site generalisation (cross-dataset transfer remains weak, mIoU$_{\text{fg}}$ $\leq$ 18.7\%).
The rescue experiments (Section~\ref{sec:rescue}) use single seeds due to computational constraints; multi-seed confirmation would strengthen the negative findings.
Under the evaluated LoRA configurations, directly fine-tuning SAM or DINOv2 underperforms task-specific architectures, but the comparison is limited by the absence of hyperparameter tuning for LoRA baselines.
Additional analyses including loss exploration, ensemble analysis, engineering quantities, and deployment workflow are provided in the Supplementary Material.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We investigated the protocol sensitivity and failure mechanisms of heterogeneous dual-teacher knowledge distillation for concrete dam defect segmentation.
Our findings reveal a cautionary pattern: DTKD shows a robust +1.5~mIoU$_{\text{fg}}$ gain on the original random split, but the student no longer surpasses its best teacher on a leakage-free balanced group split, demonstrating that evaluation protocol alone can reverse method rankings on patch-level datasets.
A pixel-level oracle analysis on the validation set confirms +8.2~mIoU$_{\text{fg}}$ complementarity between the two teachers, proving the knowledge exists---but selective distillation based on pixel-level confidence fails to transfer it, because max-probability does not correlate with geometric quality.
Separately, two post-hoc interventions that do not involve Teacher~2---continued GT fine-tuning and group-aware resampling---also fail to meaningfully improve the strong Teacher~1 initialisation, suggesting that the checkpoint resists further refinement on the same small dataset.
A parameter-matched branch comparison further shows no measurable benefit from geometric inductive bias.

These negative results carry practical implications: (i)~patch-level infrastructure datasets require group-disjoint splits to produce reliable method comparisons; (ii)~oracle complementarity does not guarantee extractable distillation gains; and (iii)~the assumption that fine-tuning from a strong checkpoint always helps does not hold on small datasets.
Despite the DTKD ranking reversal, the student model (51~FPS, 28.7M parameters) remains competitive on both evaluation protocols, offering a practical baseline for automated dam inspection.
