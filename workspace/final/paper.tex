\title{TopoDistill: Topology-Aware Crack and Spalling Segmentation in Concrete Dams via Dynamic Snake Convolution and Dual-Teacher Distillation}

\author[inst1]{Yanlin Chen\corref{cor1}}
\cortext[cor1]{Corresponding author.}

\begin{highlights}
\item Dynamic Snake Convolution improves boundary quality (BF1$_{\text{cr}}$ +1.0, BF1$_{\text{fg}}$ +2.0) and spalling centreline preservation (clDice$_{\text{fg}}$ +3.0, driven by clDice$_{\text{sp}}$ +6.0; clDice$_{\text{cr}}$ is unchanged) with only 1.32M parameters. CompR$_{\text{cr}}$ increases by +2.8, but a standard Conv control also achieves +2.5 (albeit with a different parameter count and severe mIoU degradation), so the evidence for DSConv's unique geometric contribution to crack component recall is inconclusive.
\item TopoDistill's student training standard deviation is ${\sim}$2.8$\times$ lower than SegFormer-B2 (mIoU$_{\text{fg}}$ std 0.52 vs.\ 1.47), though this measures student variability with fixed teachers, not full pipeline variability. DSConv alone accounts for most of this reduction (std 0.70). DTKD yields +1.9 mIoU$_{\text{fg}}$ on the original split, but this gain does not replicate on a feature-cluster split ($-$0.6), indicating split dependence.
\item SRL improves teacher utility for downstream distillation (+1.2 mIoU$_{\text{fg}}$) but harms student training under DTKD ($-$1.1), revealing asymmetric training dynamics.
\item In zero-shot cross-dataset transfer (DamSegment$\to$S2DS), the DSConv-equipped architecture is associated with the largest share of the mIoU$_{\text{fg}}$ gain (0.1\%$\to$14.9\% out of a total 18.7\%), though the decomposition is confounded by simultaneous changes across pipeline steps (Section~\ref{sec:cross_dataset}).
\item Systematic loss exploration reveals a persistent crack--spalling tradeoff: no loss-based or sampling-based strategy surpasses the base CE+Dice on mIoU$_{\text{fg}}$.
\end{highlights}

\begin{abstract}
Automated semantic segmentation of cracks and spalling on concrete dam surfaces is essential for structural health monitoring, yet existing methods optimise pixel-level objectives without preserving the structural continuity of detected defects.
We propose \textbf{TopoDistill}, a segmentation framework that augments a SegFormer-B2 backbone with two complementary enhancements:
(1)~a \emph{Dynamic Snake Convolution} (DSConv) branch that performs deformable sampling along elongated structures, adding only 1.32M parameters; and
(2)~\emph{Dual-Teacher Knowledge Distillation} (DTKD) that transfers complementary knowledge from a task-specific teacher and a foundation-model teacher, at zero additional inference cost.
On the \textbf{DamSegment} dataset \citep{damsegment2026} (1{,}500 images, 3~classes), TopoDistill achieves 72.3\% foreground mIoU---the highest among all compared methods---with DSConv improving boundary quality (BF1$_{\text{fg}}$ +2.0) and spalling centreline preservation (clDice$_{\text{fg}}$ +3.0, primarily from spalling; clDice$_{\text{cr}}$ unchanged) and DTKD contributing +1.9 mIoU$_{\text{fg}}$ on the original split, though this gain exhibits split dependence ($-$0.6 on a feature-cluster split).
The student's cross-seed standard deviation is ${\sim}$2.8$\times$ lower than the baseline (with fixed teachers; DSConv accounts for most of the reduction).
An investigation of Skeleton Recall Loss reveals an asymmetric training dynamic: SRL improves teacher utility for downstream distillation but harms direct student optimisation under DTKD.
Zero-shot cross-dataset evaluation (DamSegment$\to$S2DS) shows that the DSConv-equipped architecture is associated with the largest share of the cross-domain mIoU$_{\text{fg}}$ gain, though the decomposition is confounded by simultaneous pipeline changes.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Concrete dams are safety-critical infrastructure whose structural integrity must be continuously monitored throughout multi-decade service lives.
Surface defects---predominantly cracks and spalling---serve as early indicators of internal deterioration, yet their manual inspection remains labour-intensive, subjective, and logistically constrained by the scale and accessibility of dam faces \citep{chen2024damsurvey,li2024dam}.
Automated semantic segmentation of these defects from high-resolution imagery therefore promises substantial gains in inspection efficiency and repeatability.

Recent deep-learning methods have advanced pixel-level defect segmentation considerably.
Encoder--decoder architectures such as U-Net \citep{ronneberger2015unet} and hierarchical transformers like SegFormer \citep{xie2021segformer} achieve competitive Intersection-over-Union (IoU) on benchmark crack datasets \citep{zou2019deepcrack,liu2019deepcrack}.
However, IoU and related region-overlap metrics are insensitive to topological errors: a prediction that fragments a continuous crack into disconnected segments may still score favourably on IoU while misrepresenting the structural extent of the defect \citep{pantoja2024cracktopology}.
For structural safety assessment, the \emph{connectivity} of a predicted crack is as important as its pixel coverage---a fragmented prediction can lead an engineer to underestimate propagation length and thus misjudge residual capacity.

Topology-preserving losses such as clDice \citep{shit2021cldice} and persistent-homology penalties \citep{hu2019topology,clough2020topological} partially address this gap, yet they either incur prohibitive GPU memory for high-resolution dam images or operate on binary masks without multi-class support.
The recently proposed Skeleton Recall Loss (SRL) \citep{kirchhoff2024skeleton} overcomes both limitations by computing recall on CPU-extracted ground-truth skeletons with over 90\% reduction in computational overhead.
Concurrently, Dynamic Snake Convolution (DSConv) \citep{qi2023dynamic} reshapes kernel sampling grids to follow elongated, tortuous paths---a morphological prior that aligns naturally with the geometry of cracks.

Meanwhile, large vision foundation models such as SAM \citep{kirillov2023sam} and DINOv2 \citep{oquab2024dinov2} offer powerful pretrained representations, yet in our experiments, parameter-efficient fine-tuning via LoRA \citep{hu2022lora} on small industrial datasets underperforms fully fine-tuned task-specific architectures under the configurations we evaluated.
A natural question arises: \emph{can the complementary strengths of task-specific and foundation models be combined}?
We address this through knowledge distillation, where a student model learns from both a task-specific teacher and a foundation-model teacher.

In this paper we propose \textbf{TopoDistill}, a framework that augments SegFormer-B2 with a parallel DSConv branch and dual-teacher knowledge distillation (DTKD). We additionally investigate Skeleton Recall Loss (SRL) and uncover an asymmetric role in distillation: SRL enriches teacher training but harms the student when combined with DTKD.
We evaluate on the semantic segmentation subset of the DamSegment dataset \citep{damsegment2026}, comprising 1{,}500 annotated dam surface images.
Our contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item A segmentation framework combining a parallel Dynamic Snake Convolution branch (1.32M parameters) with dual-teacher knowledge distillation (DTKD, zero inference overhead). DSConv improves boundary quality (BF1$_{\text{cr}}$ +1.0, BF1$_{\text{fg}}$ +2.0) and spalling centreline preservation (clDice$_{\text{fg}}$ +3.0, driven entirely by clDice$_{\text{sp}}$; clDice$_{\text{cr}}$ is unchanged), and contributes to cross-seed stability (std 1.47$\to$0.70). Contrary to our initial hypothesis, DSConv does not measurably improve crack-specific centreline Dice; its unique geometric contribution beyond standard convolution is limited (Section~\ref{sec:ablation}). DTKD adds +1.9 mIoU$_{\text{fg}}$ on the original split, though this gain does not replicate on a feature-cluster split ($-$0.6; Table~\ref{tab:group_split}). Equal-weight and per-class weighted ensembles achieve comparable results; agreement-aware pixel-level modulation is explored as an extension (Section~\ref{sec:ablation}).
    \item An empirical finding that Skeleton Recall Loss (SRL) exhibits an \emph{asymmetric} effect in knowledge distillation: SRL improves \emph{teacher} utility for downstream distillation (students trained from SRL teachers achieve +1.2 mIoU$_{\text{fg}}$) but harms direct \emph{student} optimisation when combined with DTKD ($-$1.1 mIoU$_{\text{fg}}$), revealing that optimal training recipes differ between teachers and distillation students.
    \item A comprehensive evaluation comprising six baselines (including SAM-LoRA and DINOv2-LoRA), per-tier difficulty analysis, multi-seed stability testing, and a feature-cluster split as a partial sanity check for visual near-duplicate leakage (though the different split ratio and test-set composition limit its diagnostic power; see Section~\ref{sec:discussion}). Under the evaluated LoRA configurations, task-specific design with knowledge distillation outperforms direct foundation-model fine-tuning on this small-scale industrial dataset.
    \item Empirical analyses of training strategies and cross-domain transfer: loss exploration reveals a persistent crack--spalling tradeoff at shared class boundaries, and zero-shot evaluation on S2DS shows that the DSConv-equipped architecture is associated with the largest share of cross-domain transfer improvement, though the decomposition is confounded by simultaneous pipeline changes (Section~\ref{sec:cross_dataset}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Concrete defect segmentation}
\label{sec:related:defect}

Early CNN-based crack detectors such as CrackNet \citep{zhang2017cracknet} treated defect identification as pixel-level classification without pooling layers.
Fully convolutional designs subsequently enabled dense pixel-wise prediction: DeepCrack \citep{liu2019deepcrack} introduced deeply-supervised side outputs, while \citet{zou2019deepcrack} exploited hierarchical features with multi-scale fusion.

Transformer-based architectures have demonstrated improved global context modelling.
CrackFormer \citep{liu2021crackformer} embeds self-attention to capture long-range crack continuity, and SegFormer \citep{xie2021segformer} provides a lightweight hierarchical encoder with an all-MLP decoder.
Universal architectures such as Mask2Former \citep{cheng2022mask2former} further push segmentation accuracy on general benchmarks but have seen limited adoption for infrastructure defects due to annotation scarcity.

Applied to dam inspection specifically, \citet{arafin2024concrete} performed multi-class concrete defect segmentation using DeepLabV3+, while \citet{li2024dam} proposed a lightweight CNN-Transformer hybrid for dam crack patrol inspection.
\citet{chen2024damsurvey} surveyed visual perception methods for intelligent dam inspection, identifying limited dataset scale and poor topology preservation as key open challenges.
More recently, CCT~Net \citep{gao2026cctnet} combined CNN and Transformer branches for dam surface crack segmentation, achieving 88.7\% mIoU on a custom single-class dataset, though it does not address multi-class segmentation or topology preservation.

\subsection{Topology-aware segmentation}
\label{sec:related:topology}

\citet{hu2019topology} introduced a differentiable topological loss based on persistent homology.
\citet{clough2020topological} extended persistent-homology supervision to cardiac imaging.
However, persistent-homology computation scales poorly to high-resolution images.

For tubular structures, \citet{shit2021cldice} proposed clDice, a differentiable soft-skeleton metric, but it requires GPU-based skeletonisation with substantial memory overhead.
The Skeleton Recall Loss \citep{kirchhoff2024skeleton} pre-computes ground-truth skeletons on CPU and formulates a recall objective, achieving superior connectivity with over 90\% memory reduction relative to clDice.
Recent work has critically examined topology-aware losses: \citet{arora2025srlcritique} question whether SRL's benefits generalise beyond the original evaluation setting, and \citet{joo2026cts} provide a faithful implementation of the Crack Topology Score with extensions for handling common prediction artifacts.

Our work evaluates SRL in the multi-class dam defect setting, revealing an asymmetric effect: SRL improves teacher utility for downstream distillation but harms student training when combined with soft-label objectives---consistent with the growing evidence that topology-aware losses require careful integration.

\subsection{Deformable and dynamic convolutions}
\label{sec:related:deformable}

Deformable Convolutional Networks (DCN) \citep{dai2017deformable} introduced learnable offsets for adaptive sampling.
Dynamic Snake Convolution (DSConv) \citep{qi2023dynamic} constrains offsets along a one-dimensional path, forcing snake-like sampling that traces elongated structures.
DSConv has since been adopted for various crack detection tasks: \citet{pan2025tunneldsconv} applied it to metro tunnel lining crack detection, and \citet{yu2024dscformer} proposed a dual-branch architecture combining enhanced DSConv with SegFormer for road crack segmentation, achieving competitive IoU on Crack3238 and FIND datasets.

Our work differs from \citet{yu2024dscformer} in three aspects: (1)~we address multi-class segmentation (crack \emph{and} spalling) rather than binary crack detection; (2)~we introduce dual-teacher knowledge distillation (DTKD) with optional agreement-aware pixel-level modulation, which is absent from their architecture-only approach; and (3)~we target dam-specific inspection with boundary and centreline evaluation (BF1, clDice, CompR) and cross-dataset transfer analysis.

\subsection{Knowledge distillation for segmentation}
\label{sec:related:kd}

Knowledge distillation (KD) \citep{hinton2015distilling} transfers knowledge from a teacher model to a smaller student by matching softened output distributions.
In semantic segmentation, structured KD approaches \citep{liu2019structured,wang2020intra} distil spatial relationships rather than just per-pixel logits.
Multi-teacher distillation \citep{you2017learning,yuan2021multihead} combines knowledge from multiple specialised teachers, but typically uses uniform teacher weighting across all classes.
\citet{zhang2022camkd} proposed confidence-aware multi-teacher KD (CA-MKD) with sample-wise teacher reliability weighting, though at the sample level rather than per-class or per-pixel. Our agreement-aware variant operates at the pixel level, using inter-teacher KL divergence rather than individual teacher confidence, which better captures spatial ambiguity at defect boundaries.
Recently, \citet{wang2025taskspecifickd} demonstrated task-specific KD from vision foundation models for medical segmentation, showing that distilling SAM's knowledge into lightweight students outperforms direct fine-tuning---a finding consistent with our results in the infrastructure domain.

Our dual-teacher strategy differs by combining a task-specific teacher with a foundation-model teacher whose complementary strengths are empirically validated. Ablation shows that the dual-teacher ensemble itself is the key contributor, with per-class vs.\ equal weighting yielding negligible difference. Pixel-level agreement-aware modulation is explored as an extension.
This contrasts with CA-MKD's sample-level weighting and with \citet{wang2025taskspecifickd}'s single-teacher approach.

\subsection{Foundation models for segmentation}
\label{sec:related:foundation}

The Segment Anything Model (SAM) \citep{kirillov2023sam} and DINOv2 \citep{oquab2024dinov2} represent powerful vision foundation models.
Parameter-efficient fine-tuning via LoRA \citep{hu2022lora} enables adaptation with minimal trainable parameters (${\sim}5\%$).
While promising on natural images, their effectiveness on small-scale industrial datasets remains underexplored.
\citet{ye2024samdefect} applied SAM-based instance segmentation for structural damage detection, demonstrating SAM's potential for infrastructure inspection but also noting the need for domain-specific adaptation.
In our experiments, using foundation models as teachers in knowledge distillation outperforms direct LoRA fine-tuning under the configurations evaluated---a strategy that preserves inference efficiency while inheriting the foundation model's structural knowledge, though the comparison is limited by the absence of systematic hyperparameter tuning for the LoRA baselines.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Overview}

Fig.~\ref{fig:architecture} illustrates the TopoDistill framework. Our approach enhances a SegFormer-B2 backbone at two complementary levels: (1)~\emph{feature extraction} via a parallel DSConv branch for deformable sampling along elongated structures, and (2)~\emph{knowledge transfer} via dual-teacher distillation (DTKD) to combine the strengths of task-specific and foundation models.

    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.92\textwidth]{figures/fig_architecture.png}
    \caption{Overview of the TopoDistill framework. (a)~A SegFormer-B2 encoder with a parallel CrackSnake branch (DSConv) produces 3-class segmentation logits; the DSConv output additively enhances only the crack channel. (b)~Dual-teacher knowledge distillation (DTKD): Teacher~1 (DSConv+SRL, strong on pixel accuracy) and Teacher~2 (SAM-LoRA+SRL, strong on spalling connectivity) are combined via an ensemble (equal-weight recommended; per-class variant used in reported experiments---see Section~\ref{sec:dtkd}). The dashed path shows an optional agreement-aware variant (explored in Section~\ref{sec:ablation}) that modulates the KD loss by pixel-level teacher agreement; this is \emph{not} part of the default pipeline (row~c).}
    \label{fig:architecture}
\end{figure*}

\subsection{SegFormer-B2 backbone}

We adopt SegFormer-B2 \citep{xie2021segformer} as our backbone, providing a hierarchical Mix Transformer (MiT) encoder with four stages at resolutions $H/4$, $H/8$, $H/16$, and $H/32$ with channel dimensions 64, 128, 320, and 512. The all-MLP decoder fuses multi-scale features via linear projection and upsampling. We initialise from weights pretrained on ADE20K at $512{\times}512$.

\subsection{Dynamic Snake Convolution branch}
\label{sec:dsconv}

Standard convolutions sample on fixed rectangular grids, which may be poorly matched to thin, curvilinear structures. Inspired by \citet{qi2023dynamic}, we introduce a parallel \emph{CrackSnakeBranch} that performs deformable sampling along elongated geometry. The branch targets the crack channel specifically; however, as discussed in Section~\ref{sec:ablation}, the empirical evidence for crack-specific topology improvement is limited (clDice$_{\text{cr}}$ unchanged), while the branch does improve boundary quality (BF1) and, unexpectedly, spalling centreline preservation.

\paragraph{Stage fusion.}
The branch consumes the second and third encoder stages (0-indexed: \texttt{hidden\_states[1]} at $H/8$, 128 channels, and \texttt{hidden\_states[2]} at $H/16$, 320 channels). These are output as flattened token sequences $(B, N_i, C_i)$ by the HuggingFace encoder. We reshape them to spatial tensors: $\mathbf{s}_1 \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times 128 \times H/8 \times W/8}$ and $\mathbf{s}_2 \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times 320 \times H/16 \times W/16}$.
Each is projected to a hidden dimension $d{=}64$ via a $1{\times}1$ convolution (Conv2d($128 {\to} 64$) and Conv2d($320 {\to} 64$)), then $\mathbf{s}_2$ is bilinearly upsampled to $H/8$ resolution and added element-wise:
$\mathbf{f} = \text{proj}_1(\mathbf{s}_1) + \uparrow\!\text{proj}_2(\mathbf{s}_2) \;\in\; \mathbb{R}^{B \times 64 \times H/8 \times W/8}.$

\paragraph{DSConv block.}
Two parallel DSConv blocks process $\mathbf{f}$, one deforming along the $x$-axis (morph$=$0) and one along the $y$-axis (morph$=$1).
Each block consists of three sub-steps:

\emph{(i) Offset prediction.}
A Conv2d($64 {\to} 2K{-}1, 3{\times}3$) with $K{=}9$ (zero-initialised) predicts $2K{-}1 = 17$ raw offsets per spatial location, constrained to $[-1, +1]$ (in feature-map pixel units) via $\tanh$.
The 17 positions represent a centre point plus $K{-}1 = 8$ points on each side (left/right for the $x$-path; up/down for the $y$-path).
The offsets are accumulated via cumulative summation from the kernel centre outward, so position $k$ is offset by $\sum_{j=1}^{k} \delta_j$ from the centre, where each $|\delta_j| \leq 1$~pixel. The maximum total displacement from centre is therefore $K{-}1 = 8$ feature-map pixels (i.e., 64 input pixels at $H/8$ resolution).
This cumulative construction ensures spatial continuity of the deformed sampling path.

\emph{(ii) Deformable sampling.}
For each of the 17 sampling positions, a 2D sampling grid is constructed by adding the predicted perpendicular offset (converted from pixel units to normalised $[-1,1]$ coordinates via $\delta_{\text{norm}} = \delta_{\text{px}} \times 2/(H-1)$) to the base coordinate grid, and features are extracted via \texttt{grid\_sample} with \texttt{mode="bilinear"}, \texttt{padding\_mode="zeros"}, and \texttt{align\_corners=True}.
Each sampling position produces a $(B, 64, H/8, W/8)$ feature map. The 17 sampled feature maps are concatenated \emph{channel-wise}, yielding $(B, 64 {\times} 17, H/8, W/8) = (B, 1088, H/8, W/8)$.
Note that the spatial dimensions remain unchanged---the 17 sampling positions are stacked along the channel dimension, not along a spatial axis.

\emph{(iii) Directional convolution.}
A Conv2d($1088 {\to} 64$) with a directional kernel---$(1, 9)$ for the $x$-path, $(9, 1)$ for the $y$-path---is applied to the concatenated features. Since the 17 channel slices correspond to 17 sampling positions along one spatial axis, and the directional kernel spans 9 positions along the \emph{same} spatial axis, this convolution aggregates both the deformed samples (across channels) and local spatial context (across the kernel extent). This is followed by GroupNorm(8,\,64) and ReLU.

\paragraph{Relationship to the original DSConv.}
Our implementation differs from \citet{qi2023dynamic} in that we use $2K{-}1$ sampling positions (centre plus both sides) rather than $K$ positions in one direction, and we apply a directional spatial convolution after channel concatenation rather than a $1{\times}1$ convolution. The directional kernel acts in the spatial dimension parallel to the snake path, which creates an interaction between the deformed samples (channel axis) and local neighbourhood (spatial axis) that is not present in the original formulation.

\paragraph{Path fusion and output head.}
The outputs of the two DSConv blocks are summed element-wise: $\mathbf{o} = \text{DSConv}_x(\mathbf{f}) + \text{DSConv}_y(\mathbf{f})$.
A two-layer head---Conv2d($64 {\to} 32, 3{\times}3$) + ReLU + Conv2d($32 {\to} 1, 1{\times}1$)---produces a single-channel crack enhancement map $\mathbf{E}_{\text{crack}} \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times 1 \times H/8 \times W/8}$.
The final Conv2d($32 {\to} 1$) is \emph{zero-initialised}, so the branch starts as a no-op and gradually learns crack-specific deformations during training.

\paragraph{Additive enhancement.}
$\mathbf{E}_{\text{crack}}$ is bilinearly upsampled from $H/8$ to $H/4$ (the decoder output resolution) and added \emph{only} to the crack channel logits:
\begin{equation}
    \hat{\mathbf{y}}_{\text{crack}} = \mathbf{z}_{\text{crack}} + \uparrow\!\mathbf{E}_{\text{crack}}
    \label{eq:dsconv}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{z}_{\text{crack}}$ denotes the decoder's crack logits. Spalling and background logits remain unmodified. No additional fusion parameters are required.

\paragraph{Parameter breakdown.}
The CrackSnakeBranch contains 1.32M parameters: two $1{\times}1$ projections (29K), two DSConv blocks (637K each, dominated by the directional Conv2d($1088 {\to} 64$) at 627K), and the output head (18K).
The total parameter overhead is 1.32M (+4.8\% over SegFormer-B2).

\subsection{Dual-teacher knowledge distillation}
\label{sec:dtkd}

A key observation from our experiments is that different model architectures excel at different quality dimensions: the task-specific DSConv+SRL model (Teacher~1) achieves higher pixel-level accuracy (IoU), while the foundation-model SAM-LoRA (Teacher~2) achieves higher component recall (CompR$_{\text{fg}}$), driven primarily by its substantially stronger spalling component preservation (CompR$_{\text{sp}}$ 86.0\% vs.\ 73.4\%; Table~\ref{tab:foundation}). Standard single-teacher distillation cannot exploit this complementarity.

We propose \emph{dual-teacher knowledge distillation} (DTKD) that constructs an ensemble of teacher predictions:

\paragraph{Teacher models.} We use two pretrained teachers, both trained with Skeleton Recall Loss (SRL) \citep{kirchhoff2024skeleton} to improve their utility as distillation teachers (the rationale for this choice is validated in Section~\ref{sec:ablation}). The two teachers differ in total training epochs and SRL activation schedule; full details are in Section~\ref{sec:implementation}.
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item \textbf{Teacher~1} ($T_1$): DSConv+SRL (task-specific, strong on pixel accuracy)
    \item \textbf{Teacher~2} ($T_2$): SAM-LoRA (foundation model, strong on spalling connectivity)
\end{itemize}
We select SAM-LoRA over DINOv2-LoRA as Teacher~2 because SAM-LoRA achieves substantially higher CompR$_{\text{fg}}$ (81.2\% vs.\ 76.5\%) and BF1$_{\text{fg}}$ (68.1\% vs.\ 62.4\%), providing complementary structural knowledge (Table~\ref{tab:main}). DINOv2-LoRA's advantage is limited to IoU$_{\text{sp}}$, which Teacher~1 already covers.

\paragraph{Teacher ensemble.}
In the recommended (equal-weight) configuration, the ensemble teacher logits are a convex combination:
\begin{equation}
    \tilde{\mathbf{z}} = w_1 \, \mathbf{z}_{T_1} / \tau + w_2 \, \mathbf{z}_{T_2} / \tau, \quad w_1 + w_2 = 1
    \label{eq:dtkd_ensemble_logit}
\end{equation}
with $w_1 = w_2 = 0.5$, where $\mathbf{z}_{T_i}$ are the teacher logits and $\tau$ is the softmax temperature. The student is then trained against $\tilde{\mathbf{p}} = \text{softmax}(\tilde{\mathbf{z}})$.

\paragraph{Per-class weighting variant.}
We also tested a per-class teacher weighting variant that operates in probability space. Let $p_{i,c} = \text{softmax}(\mathbf{z}_{T_i} / \tau)_c$. The per-class weighted combination is:
\begin{equation}
    q_c = w_{1,c} \cdot p_{1,c} + w_{2,c} \cdot p_{2,c}, \qquad
    \tilde{p}_c = \frac{q_c}{\sum_{k} q_k}
    \label{eq:dtkd_ensemble_perclass}
\end{equation}
where $w_{1,c} + w_{2,c} = 1$ for each class $c$. Because the per-class weights $w_{i,c}$ differ across classes, the unnormalised vector $\mathbf{q}$ does not generally sum to one; the renormalisation step alters all inter-class ratios, effectively shifting the class prior of the ensemble target relative to either teacher's original distribution.
We set $w_{2,\text{cr}} = 0.6$, $w_{2,\text{sp}} = 0.3$, $w_{2,\text{bg}} = 0.5$ (where $w_2$ denotes Teacher~2's contribution), chosen manually during early development with no systematic search.

\textbf{Important note on weight--expertise mismatch.}
These per-class weights do \emph{not} align with Teacher~2's actual strengths: Teacher~2's main advantage is spalling component recall (CompR$_{\text{sp}}$ 86.0\% vs.\ 73.4\%; Table~\ref{tab:foundation}), yet it receives only 30\% weight for spalling.
For crack, the two teachers perform identically (CompR$_{\text{cr}}$ 82.2\%), yet Teacher~2 receives 60\% weight.
The weights were set ad hoc before we fully characterised the teachers' complementary strengths, and they contradict any ``expertise-based allocation'' rationale.

Due to an oversight during development, all results reported in this paper (row~c and all downstream experiments) were trained with these per-class weights.
However, Table~\ref{tab:kd_weights} shows that equal weighting achieves comparable mIoU$_{\text{fg}}$ (72.2 vs.\ 72.3) with \emph{higher} BF1$_{\text{fg}}$ (71.5 vs.\ 70.6) and comparable CompR$_{\text{fg}}$ (83.1 vs.\ 83.0). The negligible difference confirms that DTKD's value comes from the dual-teacher ensemble itself, not from the specific weight assignment.
Equal weighting (Eq.~\ref{eq:dtkd_ensemble_logit}) is simpler, requires no manual tuning, and avoids the logical inconsistency of the per-class weights; we recommend it as the default for practitioners.

\paragraph{Distillation loss.} The student is trained with:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{KD}} = \tau^2 \cdot \text{KL}\!\left(\tilde{\mathbf{p}} \,\|\, \text{softmax}(\mathbf{z}_s / \tau)\right)
    \label{eq:kd_loss}
\end{equation}
where $\tilde{\mathbf{p}}$ is the ensemble target probability (from Eq.~\ref{eq:dtkd_ensemble_logit} or~\ref{eq:dtkd_ensemble_perclass}) and $\mathbf{z}_s$ the student logits.

\paragraph{Agreement-aware distillation.}
When two teachers with different architectures and training recipes are combined, their predictions inevitably disagree at certain pixels---particularly at ambiguous crack--background boundaries and thin structures. At such pixels the ensemble soft label is unreliable, and forcing the student to match it may introduce noise. We note that high inter-teacher agreement does not guarantee correctness---both teachers may agree on an incorrect label---but empirically, agreement-weighted pixels yield more consistent supervision than disagreement regions.
We quantify per-pixel teacher agreement using the KL divergence between the two softened teacher distributions:
\begin{equation}
    d(i) = \text{KL}\!\left(\text{softmax}(\mathbf{z}_{T_1}^{(i)} / \tau) \,\|\, \text{softmax}(\mathbf{z}_{T_2}^{(i)} / \tau)\right)
    \label{eq:teacher_disagree}
\end{equation}
where $i$ indexes spatial positions. An agreement map is derived as $a(i) = 1 - d(i) / \max_j d(j)$, ranging from 1 (full agreement) to 0 (maximum disagreement). The agreement-aware distillation loss modulates the per-pixel KD contribution:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{KD}}^{\text{conf}} = \tau^2 \cdot \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i} a(i) \cdot \text{KL}\!\left(\tilde{\mathbf{p}}^{(i)} \,\|\, \text{softmax}(\mathbf{z}_s^{(i)} / \tau)\right)
    \label{eq:conf_kd}
\end{equation}
The intent is that the student learns strongly from the ensemble where both teachers concur, but falls back on the ground-truth supervised loss at ambiguous pixels. The agreement map is computed within the existing forward pass (no additional inference cost).

\textbf{Implicit sample-level effect.}
Because the loss is normalised by pixel count $N$ (not by $\sum_i a(i)$), an image with low overall agreement has lower total KD loss magnitude than one with high agreement. This means the formulation does \emph{not} operate purely at the pixel level: it also implicitly attenuates the KD loss for high-disagreement \emph{samples}. The sample-level reweighting ablation (Section~\ref{sec:ablation}) therefore does not fully isolate pixel-level from sample-level effects---both mechanisms are entangled in Eq.~\ref{eq:conf_kd}.

\textbf{Technical limitations.}
The per-image max-normalisation $a(i) = 1 - d(i)/\max_j d(j)$ has several issues: (i)~division by zero when $\max_j d(j) = 0$ (both teachers agree everywhere; in practice this is handled by adding $\epsilon = 10^{-8}$ in the implementation); (ii)~the agreement map is scale-set by a single pixel (the most disagreeing one), making it sensitive to outliers; (iii)~KL divergence is asymmetric ($\text{KL}(T_1 \| T_2) \neq \text{KL}(T_2 \| T_1)$); (iv)~per-image normalisation makes the agreement values incomparable across samples.
Alternative designs (e.g., JS divergence, sum-normalisation $\sum_i a(i) \cdot \text{KL}_i / \sum_i a(i)$ to remove the implicit sample attenuation, exponential decay, or fixed-threshold gating) may address these issues but are left to future work.
Fig.~\ref{fig:agreement} visualises the disagreement map on representative Hard-tier samples: high-disagreement pixels (bright regions) concentrate at crack--background boundaries and thin branching points, precisely where the ensemble soft label is least reliable.
We empirically validate that teacher disagreement correlates with sample difficulty: the mean per-sample KL divergence between teachers is 43\% higher on Hard-tier images than on Easy-tier images (Section~\ref{sec:ablation}).

\paragraph{Total loss.} The primary training objective (row~c) balances the segmentation loss and distillation loss:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L} = (1-\alpha) \left[\lambda_1 \mathcal{L}_{\text{CE}} + \lambda_2 \mathcal{L}_{\text{Dice}}\right] + \alpha \, \mathcal{L}_{\text{KD}}
    \label{eq:total_loss}
\end{equation}
with $\alpha = 0.5$, $\tau = 4$, and $\lambda_1 = \lambda_2 = 0.5$.
The agreement-aware variant (row~g) substitutes $\mathcal{L}_{\text{KD}}^{\text{conf}}$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:conf_kd}) for $\mathcal{L}_{\text{KD}}$, down-weighting unreliable pixels.
Note that SRL is used only during \emph{teacher} training; the student does not use SRL, as we find it conflicts with the distillation objective (Section~\ref{sec:ablation}).

\subsection{Test-time augmentation}

At inference, we optionally employ multi-scale TTA: each image is evaluated at three scales (0.75$\times$, 1.0$\times$, 1.25$\times$) with horizontal and vertical flips (9 views). Logits are averaged before argmax.

\subsection{Dataset}

We evaluate on the \textbf{semantic segmentation subset} of the DamSegment dataset \citep{damsegment2026}, which comprises 3{,}500 image patches captured during an on-site visit of a concrete dam, supporting classification, detection, and segmentation tasks.
The segmentation subset contains 1{,}500 pixel-level annotated images with three classes (background, crack, spalling) at $512{\times}512$ resolution, organised into three difficulty tiers (Easy, Medium, Hard; 500 each) based on crack detection difficulty, enabling curriculum learning.
We split the data into train/val/test sets of 1{,}200/150/150 images, stratified by tier. Splitting is at the patch level; patches from adjacent regions may share visual characteristics (see Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).
Fig.~\ref{fig:dataset} shows representative samples from each tier with ground-truth overlays; crack pixels range from 0.3\% to 7.9\% and spalling from 1.0\% to 11.7\%, illustrating the extreme class imbalance.

\subsection{Implementation details}
\label{sec:implementation}

All models are trained on a single NVIDIA A40 GPU using PyTorch.
SegFormer-DSConv uses SegFormer-B2 pretrained on ADE20K-512, trained for 100 epochs with AdamW (lr $6{\times}10^{-5}$, weight decay $10^{-4}$), 5-epoch warmup, batch size 4.
The DSConv branch uses $d{=}64$, $K{=}9$.
Data augmentation consists of random horizontal and vertical flips ($p{=}0.5$ each); images are resized to $512{\times}512$ and normalised with ImageNet statistics.

For DTKD, both teachers are trained independently with SRL ($\lambda_3 = 0.05$) and then frozen during student training. Because the two teachers differ in total training epochs, SRL is activated at different absolute epochs to allow sufficient warm-up:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item \textbf{Teacher~1} (DSConv+SRL): 100 epochs, SRL activated at epoch 60 (i.e., 40 epochs with SRL).
    \item \textbf{Teacher~2} (SAM-LoRA+SRL): 50 epochs, SRL activated at epoch 20 (i.e., 30 epochs with SRL).
\end{itemize}
Both teachers use best-validation-mIoU$_{\text{fg}}$ checkpoint selection. The SAM-LoRA baseline in Table~\ref{tab:main} (without SRL) uses the same 50-epoch schedule and hyperparameters as Teacher~2 but with SRL disabled.
The student has identical architecture to Teacher~1 but is trained \emph{without} SRL (CE + Dice + KD only), for 100 epochs.
Distillation uses $\alpha = 0.5$, $\tau = 4$.

For foundation model baselines, we freeze the pretrained encoder and train only LoRA adapters plus a lightweight decoder.
\textbf{SAM-LoRA} uses SAM's ViT-B encoder (frozen) with LoRA (rank~16, $\alpha{=}16$) injected into all attention Q/K/V and MLP projection layers (48 layers total); we replace SAM's original prompt-conditioned mask decoder with a 4-scale FPN decoder (features from blocks [2,\,5,\,8,\,11], $d_{\text{fpn}}{=}256$, Conv$1{\times}1$ lateral projections with GroupNorm-32 + Conv$3{\times}3$ top-down fusion, sum-aggregated to the highest resolution, followed by a Conv$3{\times}3$ + GroupNorm + ReLU + Conv$1{\times}1$ segmentation head) for prompt-free semantic segmentation---no prompts are used at training or test time.
\textbf{DINOv2-LoRA} adapts ViT-B/14 (frozen) identically: LoRA (rank~16, $\alpha{=}16$) into attention Q/K/V and MLP fc1/fc2 layers, same FPN decoder architecture, input size $518{\times}518$ ($37{\times}14$ patch-size multiple).
Both use AdamW with separate learning rates for LoRA ($10^{-4}$) and decoder ($3{\times}10^{-4}$) parameters, weight decay $10^{-4}$, 5-epoch warmup, 50 epochs, batch size~2.
No hyperparameter search was conducted for the foundation model baselines; the LoRA rank, learning rates, and decoder architecture were set based on common practices in the literature without tuning on DamSegment validation data.
Consequently, comparisons between foundation model baselines and our method (Section~\ref{sec:experiments}) reflect the specific LoRA configurations evaluated, and the conclusion that indirect use via distillation outperforms direct fine-tuning should be qualified accordingly.

\subsection{Evaluation metrics}

We report complementary metrics capturing different aspects of segmentation quality:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item \textbf{mIoU$_{\text{fg}}$}: Mean IoU of crack and spalling classes.
    \item \textbf{IoU$_{\text{cr}}$}, \textbf{IoU$_{\text{sp}}$}: Per-class IoU.
    \item \textbf{BF1}: Boundary F1 score (2-pixel tolerance) \citep{csurka2013good}.
    \item \textbf{clDice}: Centreline Dice via skeletonisation \citep{shit2021cldice}.
    \item \textbf{CompR}: Component Recall---fraction of ground-truth connected components for which $\geq$50\% of pixels are correctly predicted (i.e., the predicted class mask overlaps the GT component by at least half). This is a \emph{coverage recall} metric, not a connectivity metric: a GT crack component fragmented into multiple disconnected prediction segments still counts as ``recalled'' provided the fragments collectively cover $\geq$50\% of the GT pixels. CompR does not penalise false-positive components, over-prediction, or component merging/splitting. A model predicting all pixels as a given class would achieve CompR $\approx$ 100\% for that class. CompR should always be interpreted alongside precision-sensitive metrics (BF1, IoU) and connectivity-sensitive metrics (clDice). The 50\% threshold is a single operating point; we did not perform sensitivity analysis across thresholds (e.g., 25\%, 75\%).
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{(a$'$) vs.\ (b): Standard Conv control.}
To probe whether DSConv's gains stem from its deformable sampling geometry or from the additional capacity of any parallel branch, we compare against a standard Conv control (row~a$'$) that replaces the two DSConv blocks with a standard directional convolution path: a Conv2d($64 {\to} 1088, 3{\times}3$) explicitly expands channels (matching DSConv's implicit expansion via grid sampling), followed by the same directional Conv2d($(1, K)$) and Conv2d($(K, 1)$) + GroupNorm + ReLU, and the same output head.
Both branches share identical stage projections and the same output head; they differ only in how the $64 {\to} 1088$ channel expansion is performed.

\textbf{Limitations of this control.}
The comparison has two important caveats.
First, the control is \emph{not} parameter-matched: the standard Conv branch has 1.93M parameters (46\% more than DSConv's 1.32M), because the Conv2d($64 {\to} 1088, 3{\times}3$) creates a large, dense weight matrix whereas DSConv achieves the same channel expansion implicitly via 17 grid-sample operations on 64-channel features. This parameter mismatch cuts both ways: one might argue the control has more capacity and should perform better, but the large $3{\times}3$ kernel may also be harder to optimise and more prone to overfitting on 1{,}200 training images.
Second, the standard Conv control's severe mIoU collapse ($-$4.1) may reflect an optimisation failure of its specific architecture (a very wide $3{\times}3$ kernel feeding into directional convolutions) rather than a general inability of standard convolutions to serve as a parallel branch. A fairer comparison would require multiple control designs (e.g., parameter-matched $1{\times}1$ projections, dilated convolutions, deformable convolution v2, or even a fixed-offset ``frozen snake'' branch) and multi-seed runs, none of which were performed.

With these caveats, the control provides only weak evidence:
Standard Conv \emph{degrades} overall performance relative to vanilla SegFormer: mIoU$_{\text{fg}}$ drops from 70.3 to 66.2 ($-$4.1), with IoU$_{\text{sp}}$ falling sharply ($-$7.0).
In contrast, DSConv (row~b) with fewer parameters maintains mIoU while improving boundary quality (BF1$_{\text{fg}}$ +2.0).
However, for crack-specific topology: clDice$_{\text{cr}}$ is unchanged by DSConv (81.8$\to$81.8), and CompR$_{\text{cr}}$ increases similarly for both branches (+2.5 for standard Conv, +2.8 for DSConv), suggesting that the CompR$_{\text{cr}}$ gain is an effect of adding \emph{any} parallel branch rather than DSConv's deformable geometry specifically.
We therefore cannot conclude from this single control that DSConv's advantage is due to its geometric inductive bias; the comparison shows only that this particular standard Conv architecture fails, while DSConv succeeds. More rigorous controls are needed to isolate the contribution of deformable sampling.

\paragraph{(a)$\to$(b): Adding DSConv.}
The DSConv branch produces minimal mIoU change (+0.1) but improves boundary quality: BF1$_{\text{cr}}$ +1.0, BF1$_{\text{fg}}$ +2.0, and clDice$_{\text{fg}}$ +3.0.
However, the clDice$_{\text{fg}}$ gain is driven \emph{entirely} by spalling (clDice$_{\text{sp}}$ ${\approx}$83.6$\to$89.6); crack-specific clDice$_{\text{cr}}$ is unchanged (81.8$\to$81.8).
CompR$_{\text{cr}}$ increases by +2.8 (81.0$\to$83.8), but as noted above, a standard Conv branch achieves +2.5, limiting the evidence for DSConv's unique geometric contribution to crack component recall.
DSConv's measurable crack-specific benefit is confined to modest boundary improvement (BF1$_{\text{cr}}$ +1.0); its stronger effects are on spalling metrics and overall boundary quality, which was not our initial design intent.

\paragraph{(b$_1$)--(b$_2$) vs.\ (c): Single- vs.\ dual-teacher KD.}
To verify that both teachers are necessary, we train single-teacher KD variants with identical student settings as row~(c): row~(b$_1$) uses only Teacher~1 (task-specific) and row~(b$_2$) uses only Teacher~2 (foundation model), with the other teacher's weight set to zero.
Neither single teacher matches the dual-teacher result: T1-only achieves 69.0\% mIoU$_{\text{fg}}$ and T2-only 69.2\%, both ${\sim}$3 points below dual-teacher DTKD (72.3\%) and even below the no-KD DSConv baseline (row~b, 70.4\%).
This indicates that a single teacher's soft labels, occupying 50\% of the training signal ($\alpha{=}0.5$), can introduce more noise than useful information when the teacher lacks complementary knowledge to counterbalance its biases.
The two teachers nonetheless show complementary strengths: T2-only achieves substantially higher CompR$_{\text{fg}}$ (85.0 vs.\ T1-only's 81.0), reflecting SAM-LoRA's stronger component preservation, while both show similar IoU$_{\text{cr}}$ (${\sim}$56).
Dual-teacher KD combines these complementary strengths, achieving the best mIoU$_{\text{fg}}$ and IoU$_{\text{sp}}$ while maintaining strong crack metrics.

\paragraph{(b)$\to$(c): Adding DTKD.}
DTKD provides substantial pixel-level improvement: mIoU$_{\text{fg}}$ +1.9, IoU$_{\text{cr}}$ +1.1, IoU$_{\text{sp}}$ +2.8, clDice$_{\text{cr}}$ +1.5, and BF1$_{\text{cr}}$ +0.6, while also improving component recall: CompR$_{\text{fg}}$ +2.6 (80.4$\to$83.0).
CompR$_{\text{cr}}$ decreases modestly from 83.8 to 82.1 ($-$1.7), as DTKD's soft-label supervision slightly smooths fine crack structures, but this is more than offset by substantially improved spalling component recall.
The dual-teacher ensemble ensures that the student receives complementary guidance from both teacher architectures (Section~\ref{sec:dtkd}).

\paragraph{(c)$\to$(d): Test-time augmentation.}
TTA provides modest additional gains (mIoU$_{\text{fg}}$ +0.4, IoU$_{\text{cr}}$ +0.8) but reduces component recall (CompR$_{\text{fg}}$ 83.0$\to$80.2), suggesting that multi-scale/flip aggregation improves pixel coverage but may smooth fine structural details.

\paragraph{(c)$\to$(g): Agreement-aware KD (exploratory).}
Replacing the standard KD loss with agreement-aware distillation (Eq.~\ref{eq:conf_kd}) shows boundary quality improvements in a single run: BF1$_{\text{cr}}$ +0.8 (75.6$\to$76.4) and clDice$_{\text{cr}}$ +0.6 (83.3$\to$83.9), with mIoU$_{\text{fg}}$ +0.2.
However, the mIoU gain (+0.2) falls within the multi-seed standard deviation of DTKD (0.52, Table~\ref{tab:multiseed}), and CompR$_{\text{cr}}$ decreases (82.1$\to$81.4, $-$0.7). Without multi-seed validation, these improvements should be interpreted cautiously.
The mechanism---down-weighting the distillation signal at high-disagreement pixels---is principled, but the current agreement map formulation (per-image max-normalisation) has limitations: sensitivity to outlier disagreement values and incomparability across samples.

To probe whether the gains come from pixel-level modulation or sample-level reweighting, we conducted an ablation using \emph{explicit} disagreement-based per-sample loss reweighting without pixel-level confidence modulation.
This variant degraded performance (mIoU$_{\text{fg}}$ 72.1, BF1$_{\text{fg}}$ 70.0), indicating that explicit sample reweighting hurts on small datasets.
However, as noted in Section~\ref{sec:dtkd}, the agreement-aware KD loss (Eq.~\ref{eq:conf_kd}) itself contains an \emph{implicit} sample-level attenuation (due to $1/N$ normalisation rather than $1/\sum a(i)$), so the two mechanisms are not fully disentangled. The ablation shows that explicit sample reweighting is harmful, but we cannot claim that the agreement-aware KD gains are purely pixel-level.

Analysis of teacher disagreement across difficulty tiers reveals that the mean per-sample KL divergence between teachers is 0.046 on Hard-tier images vs.\ 0.032 on Easy-tier images (+43\%), confirming that disagreement is an informative proxy for sample difficulty.
However, this correlation does not translate into effective sample-level curriculum learning---consistent with prior findings that data-restricting or reweighting curriculum strategies underperform on small datasets where every sample matters.

\paragraph{Effect of SRL (rows e--f).}
\label{sec:srl}
Skeleton Recall Loss (SRL) \citep{kirchhoff2024skeleton} penalises low predicted probability at ground-truth skeleton pixels $\mathcal{S}$, precomputed offline via morphological skeletonisation:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{SRL}} = 1 - \frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}|} \sum_{p \in \mathcal{S}} \hat{y}_{\text{crack}}(p)
    \label{eq:srl}
\end{equation}
We apply SRL only to the crack class (weight $\lambda_3 = 0.05$). For the 100-epoch DSCformer models in this table, SRL is activated at epoch 60; for the 50-epoch SAM-LoRA teacher, SRL is activated at epoch 20 (see Section~\ref{sec:implementation}).
Rows (e)--(f) show the effect of adding SRL to the pipeline.
Adding SRL to DSConv (row e) provides no crack improvement (IoU$_{\text{cr}}$ $-$0.1, BF1$_{\text{cr}}$ $-$0.3) and reduces component recall: CompR$_{\text{fg}}$ drops from 80.4 to 77.8 ($-$2.6), driven by CompR$_{\text{sp}}$ falling from 77.0 to 73.4 as SRL's crack-focused supervision fragments spalling components at ambiguous boundaries.
Adding DTKD on top of SRL (row f) partially recovers connectivity (77.8$\to$80.6) but the resulting model (f, mIoU$_{\text{fg}}$ 71.2\%) underperforms the SRL-free pipeline (c, mIoU$_{\text{fg}}$ 72.3\%) by \textbf{$-$1.1}.
This suggests an optimisation conflict between the SRL and KD objectives: the student learns better from teacher signals alone without the competing skeleton objective.
Multi-seed experiments (Table~\ref{tab:multiseed}) further confirm that the SRL path is unstable (mIoU$_{\text{fg}}$ std 2.42 vs.\ 0.70 for DSConv-only and 0.52 for DTKD).

\paragraph{Overall progression.}
From baseline (a) to the primary method TopoDistill (c) within this table, the cumulative improvement is +2.0 mIoU$_{\text{fg}}$, +2.0 BF1$_{\text{fg}}$, +3.7 clDice$_{\text{fg}}$, and +6.0 CompR$_{\text{fg}}$ (the +2.0 vs.\ +1.9 in Table~\ref{tab:main} reflects the different baseline seed). DSConv contributes boundary quality and spalling centreline preservation; DTKD contributes pixel accuracy and crack-specific clDice$_{\text{cr}}$ (+1.5, from 81.8 to 83.3) on this split (see Table~\ref{tab:group_split} for split-dependence analysis). The exploratory agreement-aware extension (g) adds modest boundary gains (+0.8 BF1$_{\text{cr}}$) that require multi-seed confirmation.

\paragraph{Statistical significance.}
We conduct two-sided paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests on the test images (150 total), comparing DSConv-only (row~b) vs.\ TopoDistill (row~c, final model).
Because not all images contain both classes in the ground truth, the effective sample size ($n$) varies by metric: crack metrics use $n{=}148$ (IoU/Dice) or $n{=}146$ (BF1, clDice, CompR), while spalling metrics use $n{=}40$ (IoU/Dice), $n{=}31$ (BF1, clDice), or $n{=}37$ (CompR).
Images where the ground-truth class is absent produce undefined per-image values (NaN) for that metric and are excluded from the test; images where both models produce identical scores (zero difference) are discarded per the default ``wilcox'' method in SciPy. SciPy automatically selects exact or asymptotic $p$-values based on sample size.
Seven of ten per-class metrics reach \emph{uncorrected} significance at $\alpha{=}0.05$: six show DTKD improvement---IoU$_{\text{cr}}$ ($p{=}0.008$, $n{=}148$), Dice$_{\text{cr}}$ ($p{=}0.007$, $n{=}148$), BF1$_{\text{cr}}$ ($p{=}0.046$, $n{=}146$), clDice$_{\text{cr}}$ ($p{<}0.001$, $n{=}146$), IoU$_{\text{sp}}$ ($p{=}0.016$, $n{=}40$), and Dice$_{\text{sp}}$ ($p{=}0.016$, $n{=}40$)---while CompR$_{\text{cr}}$ shows a significant decrease ($-$1.7, $p{=}0.040$, $n{=}146$), reflecting the connectivity trade-off discussed above. The spalling $p$-values should be interpreted cautiously given the small $n$ (31--40).
After Holm--Bonferroni correction for 10 simultaneous comparisons, only clDice$_{\text{cr}}$ ($p_{\text{adj}}{=}0.010$) remains significant at the family-wise $\alpha{=}0.05$ level; the remaining metrics show adjusted $p$-values between 0.063 and 0.30. The results consistently favour DTKD across several metrics, but the per-class IoU measures (IoU$_{\text{cr}}$, IoU$_{\text{sp}}$) do not survive multiple-comparison correction. Therefore, while the directional consistency is encouraging, we cannot claim statistically confirmed gains on these metrics based on the current test set.
Tier-stratified analysis reveals the strongest effects on Hard images: IoU$_{\text{cr}}$ ($p{=}0.003$), Dice$_{\text{cr}}$ ($p{=}0.004$), BF1$_{\text{cr}}$ ($p{=}0.045$), and clDice$_{\text{cr}}$ ($p{<}0.001$) all reach uncorrected significance, suggesting that the foundation-model teacher's global knowledge provides the strongest benefit on complex crack patterns.

\paragraph{Multi-seed training stability.}

Since Teacher~1 (DSConv+SRL) itself has high variability (std 2.42), re-training the teachers with different seeds and then re-training the student could yield substantially higher overall pipeline variability. The stability comparison between TopoDistill and other methods should be interpreted with this asymmetry in mind.

\subsection{Loss function and training strategy exploration}
\label{sec:strategy}

\paragraph{Error analysis.}
To guide loss function design, we analyse the confusion matrix of the DTKD model (row~c).
For crack pixels, precision is 68.8\% while recall is 79.7\%, yielding a false-positive to false-negative ratio of 1.78:1.
The majority of false positives occur at crack--background boundaries, where thin crack predictions leak into adjacent background pixels.
This precision bottleneck motivates exploring loss functions that penalise false positives more heavily.

\paragraph{Strategy variants.}
Table~\ref{tab:strategy} summarises six training strategies applied on top of the DTKD pipeline (row~c), targeting crack precision improvement through loss modification or sampling adjustment.
All variants use the same SegFormer-DSConv architecture with DTKD; they differ only in the segmentation loss or sampling strategy.

\paragraph{Crack--spalling tradeoff.}
A consistent pattern emerges across all loss-based strategies: improving crack boundary precision degrades spalling metrics.
Tversky loss with $\alpha{=}0.6$ (stronger FP penalty) improves BF1$_{\text{sp}}$ by +3.4 over the baseline, but reduces mIoU$_{\text{fg}}$ by $-$0.6 and CompR$_{\text{fg}}$ by $-$3.6.
WABL, which specifically targets thin-crack boundary false positives, causes the largest spalling regression (IoU$_{\text{sp}}$ $-$1.4, BF1$_{\text{sp}}$ $-$1.6).
Combining WABL with Tversky amplifies the degradation (IoU$_{\text{sp}}$ $-$2.8).
This tradeoff may arise partly from shared geometric boundaries where crack and spalling co-occur; loss terms that suppress crack false positives may inadvertently penalise adjacent spalling predictions.

\paragraph{Hard-tier fine-tuning.}
Sampling-based approaches partially mitigate this tradeoff.
Fine-tuning from the DTKD checkpoint with 70\% Hard-tier oversampling and WABL achieves BF1$_{\text{fg}}$ 72.2 (comparable to the best loss-based variant) while maintaining better spalling coverage (IoU$_{\text{sp}}$ 85.0 vs.\ 83.4--85.4 for loss variants) and the highest clDice$_{\text{fg}}$ (87.3).
However, no strategy surpasses the base DTKD model on mIoU$_{\text{fg}}$ (72.3), indicating that the standard CE+Dice combination is already near-optimal for this dataset and that the crack precision bottleneck requires fundamentally different approaches (e.g., higher-resolution inputs or explicit boundary modelling in the architecture).

\textit{DSConv$\times$DTKD interaction.}
The factorial analysis reveals a synergistic, not additive, interaction between DSConv and DTKD. DTKD improves mIoU$_{\text{fg}}$ by +1.9 when combined with DSConv but \emph{degrades} performance by $-$0.7 on the plain SegFormer backbone. This suggests that DSConv's geometric inductive bias---shared with Teacher~1's architecture---makes the student better equipped to absorb teacher knowledge. Without this architectural affinity, the student cannot effectively leverage the distillation signal.

\textit{Student surpasses ensemble ceiling.}
The DTKD student (72.3\%) surpasses the best direct teacher ensemble (70.7\%, equal logit averaging) by +1.6 mIoU$_{\text{fg}}$, confirming that distillation provides regularisation benefits beyond simple ensemble knowledge transfer. The student learns to integrate complementary teacher knowledge more effectively than a static ensemble combination.

\textit{Teacher diversity controls.}
Using a duplicated teacher (67.5\%) produces the worst result among all configurations, $-$4.8 below DTKD and $-$2.9 below the DSConv-only baseline, confirming that mere soft-target training without diversity is harmful. Using two same-architecture teachers with different seeds (70.4\%) exactly matches the DSConv-only baseline, indicating that seed diversity alone provides no benefit---architectural complementarity between the heterogeneous teacher pair (DSConv+SRL and SAM-LoRA+SRL) is what drives DTKD's gains.

\textit{Not soft-target regularisation.}
Label smoothing (68.7\%) underperforms the DSConv-only baseline (70.4\%) by $-$1.7, ruling out generic soft-target regularisation as the mechanism behind DTKD's improvement. The benefit derives from actual teacher knowledge, not from softer training signals per se.

\paragraph{Teacher selection ablation.}
Since the DTKD student does not use SRL, one might expect a teacher trained \emph{without} SRL (higher CompR$_{\text{fg}}$) to provide cleaner knowledge transfer.
We test this by replacing Teacher~1 (DSConv+SRL, CompR$_{\text{fg}}$ 77.8\%) with the SRL-free DSConv-only variant (CompR$_{\text{fg}}$ 80.4\%).
The resulting student achieves 71.1\% mIoU$_{\text{fg}}$, \textbf{$-$1.2} below the G1 teacher variant (72.3\%).
The degradation concentrates on spalling (IoU$_{\text{sp}}$ $-$2.4, CompR$_{\text{sp}}$ $-$1.4), while crack metrics are largely unaffected.
This indicates that SRL-trained Teacher~1 produces more useful soft supervision for the student---despite its own connectivity regression---that transfers more effectively through distillation.
Teacher test performance alone does not predict downstream distillation utility.

\subsection{Cross-dataset evaluation}
\label{sec:cross_dataset}

To assess generalisability beyond the training domain, we perform zero-shot transfer evaluation on the S2DS dataset \citep{s2ds2021}, which contains 743 images ($1024{\times}1024$) of concrete surfaces with pixel-level annotations for seven defect classes: background, crack, spalling, corrosion, efflorescence, vegetation, and control point.
We map S2DS crack and spalling to our label scheme and treat all other classes as background, since they fall outside the DamSegment label space.
All images are resized to $512{\times}512$ (bilinear for images, nearest-neighbour for masks).
The S2DS test split contains 93~images, of which 27 contain only crack, 20 contain only spalling, and 46 are background-only; crack pixels account for 0.12\% and spalling for 4.27\% of the test area.
No fine-tuning is performed: models trained on DamSegment are applied directly.

Table~\ref{tab:cross_dataset} reveals a large domain gap for all models. SegFormer-B2 and Mask2Former fail almost completely on the unseen domain (mIoU$_{\text{fg}}$ $<$2.5\%). SegFormer-B2's near-zero scores likely reflect class collapse (predicting nearly all pixels as one class) rather than informative misclassification; we did not examine per-class prediction pixel proportions or confusion matrices to diagnose the failure mode. All DSConv-equipped variants substantially outperform the vanilla baselines, achieving 14.9--18.7\% mIoU$_{\text{fg}}$, though the absolute values remain limited, reflecting the inherent difficulty of zero-shot cross-domain segmentation under significant distribution shift. TopoDistill achieves the highest mIoU$_{\text{fg}}$ (18.7\%; TTA does not improve this, yielding 18.6\%), while DSConv+SRL (Teacher~1) achieves the highest BF1$_{\text{fg}}$ (49.6\%) and clDice$_{\text{fg}}$ (72.1\%).

The DSConv-only model (row~b, no SRL or KD) achieves 14.9\% mIoU$_{\text{fg}}$, a +14.8 gain over SegFormer-B2's near-zero transfer (0.1\%). The subsequent steps show DSConv+SRL at 17.1\% (+2.2) and TopoDistill at 18.7\% (+1.6).

\textbf{Caveats on this decomposition.}
This is \emph{not} a clean additive component ablation, for several reasons:
(i)~The final step (DSConv+SRL $\to$ TopoDistill) simultaneously \emph{adds} DTKD and \emph{removes} student SRL; the +1.6 therefore conflates DTKD's contribution with the effect of dropping SRL from the student, and cannot be attributed to DTKD alone.
(ii)~SegFormer-B2's near-zero transfer (0.1\%) likely reflects class collapse (predicting nearly all pixels as one class) rather than informative misclassification; the large baseline gap inflates the apparent contribution of any subsequent modification.
(iii)~The standard Conv control was not evaluated on S2DS, so we cannot separate DSConv's deformable geometry from the effect of any additional parallel branch.
(iv)~DSConv modifies only the crack logit channel, yet the largest cross-domain gains appear in spalling IoU (0.0 $\to$ 23.1 for DSConv, vs.\ 0.1 $\to$ 6.8 for crack), which suggests the benefit operates through indirect mechanisms (e.g., improved encoder representations via gradient flow) rather than DSConv's geometric sampling directly.
(v)~All S2DS results are single-seed; without multi-seed runs or bootstrap confidence intervals, the ordering may not be robust.

We can state that the DSConv-equipped architecture is \emph{associated with} substantially better cross-domain transfer, but the decomposition does not support strong causal claims about individual component contributions.
Unlike in-domain evaluation, TTA does not improve cross-dataset performance (18.7\%$\to$18.6\%), suggesting that multi-scale aggregation offers diminishing returns under large domain shift.

Fig.~\ref{fig:cross_dataset} shows representative S2DS predictions. SegFormer-B2 and Mask2Former produce largely incorrect segmentations, while DSConv+SRL and TopoDistill localise defect regions despite the domain shift, consistent with the quantitative results above.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{DTKD: benefits and limitations.}
On the original split, DTKD adds +1.9 mIoU$_{\text{fg}}$ over DSConv alone. A $2{\times}2$ factorial analysis reveals that DTKD's benefit depends on DSConv: without DSConv, DTKD degrades performance ($-$0.7 mIoU$_{\text{fg}}$), indicating a synergistic rather than additive interaction (Table~\ref{tab:factorial}). The student surpasses the best direct teacher ensemble by +1.6 mIoU$_{\text{fg}}$, confirming that distillation provides regularisation benefits beyond simple ensemble knowledge transfer. TopoDistill's student training standard deviation is ${\sim}$2.8$\times$ lower than the SegFormer baseline (mIoU$_{\text{fg}}$ std 0.52 vs.\ 1.47, Table~\ref{tab:multiseed}), though this measures student variability with fixed teachers, not full pipeline variability. DSConv alone already achieves most of this reduction (std 0.70); DTKD's additional contribution to stability (0.70$\to$0.52) is marginal given only three seeds. Furthermore, the mIoU$_{\text{fg}}$ gain does not replicate on the feature-cluster split ($-$0.6, Table~\ref{tab:group_split}), though this reversal is confounded by the different test-set composition and halved training set (see caveats in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}). The multi-seed means on the original split differ by only 1.0 point (DSConv-only 70.8\% vs.\ TopoDistill 71.8\%), a more modest gap than the single-seed +1.9 suggests. DTKD's clearest contribution is in cross-dataset transfer (+1.6 mIoU$_{\text{fg}}$ on S2DS, atop SRL's +2.2). We note that the stability observations are based on three seeds and do not constitute evidence for a specific mechanism (e.g., loss landscape smoothing); additional seeds and loss landscape analysis would be needed to support mechanistic claims.

\paragraph{DTKD hyperparameter sensitivity check.}
We performed a star search over the two key DTKD hyperparameters: softmax temperature $\tau \in \{2, 4, 6\}$ (fixing $\alpha{=}0.5$) and KD loss weight $\alpha \in \{0.3, 0.5, 0.7\}$ (fixing $\tau{=}4$), where $\alpha$ is the distillation weight in Eq.~\eqref{eq:total_loss}. Table~\ref{tab:hyper} reports validation-set mIoU$_{\text{fg}}$ (used for checkpoint selection) for each configuration (single run; different random seeds). Lower temperature ($\tau{=}2$) produced sharper teacher signals that degraded val mIoU$_{\text{fg}}$ to 69.7\%; higher temperature ($\tau{=}6$) achieved 71.4\%, marginally above the default's 71.1\%; shifting $\alpha$ in either direction yielded 70.2--71.0\%. The default and $\tau{=}6$ are within 0.3\%, well within the seed noise observed in Table~\ref{tab:multiseed} (std $\approx$ 0.5), so we retain the commonly used $\tau{=}4$ without claiming optimality. $\tau{=}2$ produced the lowest validation result in this limited single-run check ($-$1.4\% vs.\ default), consistent with excessively sharp teacher signals hurting, though the effect is confounded with seed variation. Overall, the results indicate reasonable local robustness within the examined range, though the search covers only five points and each is a single run with a different random seed.

\paragraph{Granularity of difficulty-aware modulation.}
Inter-teacher disagreement provides a free quality signal that correlates with sample difficulty (+43\% KL divergence on Hard vs.\ Easy). Leveraging this signal at the \emph{pixel level} (agreement-aware KD) shows promising single-run results on boundary metrics, while using it for \emph{sample-level} curriculum learning degrades performance. This suggests that on small datasets, every sample is needed for generalisation, but noisy pixels within a sample may be safely down-weighted---though multi-seed validation is needed to confirm that the pixel-level gains exceed random variation.

\paragraph{SRL: useful for teachers, harmful for students.}
SRL leads the teacher to produce more useful soft supervision that transfers well through distillation, even though SRL degrades the teacher's own connectivity metrics. As a student training component, however, SRL interferes with DTKD's soft-label supervision. This asymmetry---that optimal teacher and student training recipes differ---has broader implications for distillation pipeline design.

\paragraph{Indirect use of foundation models.}
Under the evaluated LoRA configurations, directly fine-tuning SAM or DINOv2 underperforms task-specific architectures by 2--4\% mIoU$_{\text{fg}}$. Using them as \emph{teachers} in knowledge distillation yields better results in our experiments, though this comparison is limited by the absence of hyperparameter tuning for the foundation model baselines (see Section~\ref{sec:experiments}).

\paragraph{Crack--spalling tradeoff.}
The persistent tradeoff across all loss-based strategies (Section~\ref{sec:strategy}) arises from shared geometric boundaries between defect types. Pixel-level loss modification alone cannot resolve the crack precision bottleneck without collateral damage to spalling, suggesting that sampling-based, architectural, or post-processing approaches (e.g., class-specific CRF refinement) are more promising.

\paragraph{Practical implications.}
The student model has identical inference cost to SegFormer-DSConv (96 FPS, 28.7M parameters), with teachers used only during training. On the original split, the per-tier analysis shows DTKD improves all tiers, with the largest mIoU$_{\text{fg}}$ gain on Easy (+3.4) and the strongest crack-specific significance on Hard (uncorrected $p$-values), though the overall DTKD gain exhibits split dependence (Table~\ref{tab:group_split}).

\paragraph{Failure modes and limitations.}
The model underperforms on (i)~thin hairline cracks (${<}$2 pixels wide) and (ii)~co-occurring crack--spalling regions where it over-predicts one class at the expense of the other.
The DamSegment dataset comprises images from a single dam site with patch-level splitting; spatially adjacent patches may share visual characteristics, potentially inflating in-domain metrics. To partially assess visual near-duplicate leakage, we constructed a \emph{feature-cluster split} after all design decisions were locked on the original split: ResNet-50 features were extracted for all 1{,}500 patches, agglomerative clustering (cosine distance, 100 groups) ensured visually similar patches stay in the same fold, and clusters were allocated to splits with per-tier stratification.
The cluster test set was not observed during model design or hyperparameter tuning. All models were retrained independently from the prescribed pretrained initialisations (ADE20K for SegFormer, ImageNet for SAM/DINOv2) on this split and evaluated on the corresponding test set.

\paragraph{Important caveats.}
The cluster split differs substantially from the original split in both size and composition. The original split is 1{,}200/150/150 (train/val/test) with perfectly balanced tiers (50/50/50 per tier in test); the cluster split is 631/369/500 with imbalanced tiers (test: 107 Easy / 205 Medium / 188 Hard). Because clusters are atomic allocation units, the target 80/10/10 ratio could not be achieved, resulting in an effective 42/25/33 split. Consequently, the two test sets are \emph{not directly comparable}: the cluster test set is 3.3$\times$ larger, has different tier composition, and the training set is nearly halved. Table~\ref{tab:group_split} reports results on both splits; five of six models score \emph{higher} on the cluster split despite the smaller training set. The reason is currently unknown: the na\"ive hypothesis that more Medium-tier images inflate scores is contradicted by Table~\ref{tab:per_tier}, which shows Medium has the \emph{lowest} per-image mIoU$_{\text{fg}}$ among all tiers (56--59\%). Possible explanations include differences in per-class pixel prevalence, number of positive images, component size distribution, or train--test visual similarity between the two splits, but we did not compute these diagnostics. Higher cluster-split scores therefore cannot be interpreted as evidence for or against leakage.

What Table~\ref{tab:group_split} \emph{can} show is that models do not collapse under the cluster split---performance remains in a comparable range ($\pm$3 percentage points). However, relative rankings are \emph{not} preserved for DTKD: on the cluster split, DSConv (73.3), DSConv+SRL (73.1), and SegFormer-B2 (73.0) all surpass TopoDistill (72.7) in mIoU$_{\text{fg}}$, reversing the original-split ranking where TopoDistill led. DTKD also loses its BF1$_{\text{cr}}$ and clDice$_{\text{cr}}$ advantages. DSConv-family models remain competitive across both splits, but DTKD's ranking advantage is not preserved. What the table \emph{cannot} show is that near-duplicate leakage is absent from the original split, because the higher cluster-split scores are confounded by test-set difficulty differences.

This feature-cluster split also addresses only \emph{visual} near-duplicates, not true \emph{spatial} leakage: patch coordinates, source image IDs, and dam-face regions were unavailable, so spatially adjacent patches with different visual appearances may still span train and test. A controlled comparison would require (i)~matching the split ratio to 80/10/10, (ii)~reporting per-split statistics: tier distribution, per-class pixel prevalence, number of positive images per class, connected component count and mean component size, (iii)~reporting per-class global IoU (not just mIoU$_{\text{fg}}$) to diagnose which class drives score differences, (iv)~comparing train--test nearest-neighbour feature similarity between splits, and (v)~ideally using coordinate-based or source-image-based grouping. Absent such metadata and controls, this experiment should be interpreted as a \emph{partial sanity check} rather than a definitive leakage assessment.
The S2DS cross-dataset transfer provides complementary evidence: all models face a substantial domain gap (${\sim}$19\% mIoU$_{\text{fg}}$), confirming that the learned representations do not trivially transfer and that zero-shot cross-site generalisation remains far from solved.

\paragraph{Test-set exposure during development.}
All ablation variants were evaluated on a single held-out test set. Although a separate validation set was used for hyperparameter selection and early stopping, test-set metrics were observed during model development across ablation rows. This means that design decisions---including module selection, teacher selection, loss design, and per-class weight assignment---may have been indirectly influenced by test-set performance, compromising its independence. The reported test-set differences between ablation rows (particularly small gains such as mIoU$_{\text{fg}}$ +0.1--0.2) should therefore be interpreted as upper bounds rather than unbiased estimates.
All architectural and training decisions were finalised on the original split before the feature-cluster split was constructed; the cluster test set (500 images) was not observed during model design or hyperparameter tuning. The feature-cluster sanity check (Table~\ref{tab:group_split}) shows that models do not collapse under this split, but DTKD's ranking advantage is not preserved---DSConv-family models remain competitive while TopoDistill drops from first to fourth in mIoU$_{\text{fg}}$. The two test sets differ substantially in size and tier composition (see caveats in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}), so the cluster-split results cannot rule out leakage on the original split.
Notably, DSConv achieves 73.3\% vs.\ TopoDistill's 72.7\% on the cluster test set, meaning the +1.9 mIoU$_{\text{fg}}$ advantage observed on the original split does \emph{not} replicate ($-$0.6 on the cluster split). However, this reversal is confounded by the different test-set composition and the halved training set, so it cannot be attributed solely to leakage correction.
The multi-seed analysis (Table~\ref{tab:multiseed}) provides a complementary perspective: DSConv-only and TopoDistill differ by only 1.0 point in mean mIoU$_{\text{fg}}$ (70.8\% vs.\ 71.8\%) with comparable low standard deviation (0.70 vs.\ 0.52), further suggesting that DTKD's incremental contribution is more modest than the single-seed +1.9 implies. Note that the TopoDistill standard deviation reflects student-only variability with fixed teachers (see Section~\ref{sec:ablation}).
Nonetheless, a fully rigorous protocol would require locking all design decisions on the validation set and evaluating the final model on a previously unobserved test split, or reporting repeated cross-validation with confidence intervals. We did not adopt this protocol, which is a limitation shared with most segmentation studies on fixed benchmarks but nonetheless weakens the causal interpretation of incremental ablation gains.
Per-class teacher weights are set manually; adaptive weighting is future work.
Crack IoU remains challenging (${\sim}$59\%) due to extreme class imbalance.

\paragraph{DSConv: boundary improvement without crack topology gain.}
The DSConv branch was designed to improve crack connectivity through geometry-aware deformable sampling, but our evaluation reveals a more nuanced picture. clDice$_{\text{cr}}$ is unchanged by DSConv (81.8$\to$81.8), and the CompR$_{\text{cr}}$ gain (+2.8) is similar to that of the standard Conv control (+2.5), though the control comparison is weakened by its different parameter count (1.93M vs.\ 1.32M) and severe mIoU degradation (Section~\ref{sec:ablation}). DSConv's measurable contributions are (i)~boundary quality (BF1$_{\text{cr}}$ +1.0, BF1$_{\text{fg}}$ +2.0), (ii)~spalling centreline preservation (clDice$_{\text{sp}}$ +6.0, driving the clDice$_{\text{fg}}$ +3.0), and (iii)~cross-seed stability (std 1.47$\to$0.70). The spalling benefit is unexpected given the branch targets only the crack channel logit; we hypothesise that the additional gradient flow through shared encoder features indirectly improves spalling representations, but have not verified this mechanistically.

\paragraph{Topology evaluation limitations.}
Our topology evaluation relies on clDice and CompR, both of which have limitations. clDice measures centreline overlap but is insensitive to component-level topology (e.g., a single connected prediction covering two GT components scores well on clDice but misrepresents the structural pattern). CompR is a coverage recall metric that does not penalise fragmentation, false positives, or component merging (Section~\ref{sec:metrics}). True connectivity metrics---such as the Crack Topology Score \citep{pantoja2024cracktopology}, component precision/F1, or fragmentation counts---would provide stronger evidence for or against topology preservation, but were not computed. Additionally, the CompR threshold (50\%) is a single operating point; sensitivity analysis across thresholds (25\%, 50\%, 75\%) would reveal whether the observed gains are robust or threshold-dependent. The multi-seed analysis (Table~\ref{tab:multiseed}) reports clDice$_{\text{fg}}$ and CompR$_{\text{fg}}$ but not per-class breakdowns, limiting the ability to verify whether the crack-specific null result (clDice$_{\text{cr}}$ unchanged) is consistent across seeds.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We presented TopoDistill, a segmentation framework for concrete dam defects that combines Dynamic Snake Convolution with dual-teacher knowledge distillation (DTKD).
On DamSegment, the combined framework achieves 72.3\% mIoU$_{\text{fg}}$ at 96 FPS with only 1.32M parameter overhead and no additional inference cost from distillation.
DSConv improves boundary quality (BF1$_{\text{fg}}$ +2.0) and spalling centreline preservation (clDice$_{\text{fg}}$ +3.0, driven by spalling; clDice$_{\text{cr}}$ is unchanged), and contributes to cross-seed stability (std 1.47$\to$0.70). In zero-shot cross-dataset transfer, the DSConv-equipped architecture is associated with the largest share of the mIoU$_{\text{fg}}$ gain, though the decomposition is confounded by simultaneous pipeline changes. The evidence for DSConv's unique contribution to crack-specific topology is limited: clDice$_{\text{cr}}$ shows no improvement, and a standard Conv control achieves similar CompR$_{\text{cr}}$ gains, though the control comparison is weakened by parameter mismatch and optimisation issues (Section~\ref{sec:ablation}).
DTKD adds +1.9 mIoU$_{\text{fg}}$ on the original split, though this gain exhibits split dependence ($-$0.6 on the cluster split), suggesting its incremental contribution over DSConv alone is more modest than the single-seed result implies. Control experiments confirm that DTKD's gains require architecturally diverse teachers and are not attributable to soft-target regularisation.
An investigation of Skeleton Recall Loss revealed that optimal training recipes differ between teachers and distillation students: SRL improves teacher utility but harms student optimisation under DTKD.
Exploration of alternative loss functions uncovered a persistent crack--spalling tradeoff at shared class boundaries.
Under the evaluated configurations, indirectly leveraging foundation models through knowledge distillation outperforms direct LoRA fine-tuning on this small-scale industrial dataset, though better-optimised foundation model baselines may narrow or close this gap. The DSConv-equipped architecture is associated with the largest cross-dataset transfer gains, though the decomposition is confounded and the mechanism remains unclear (Section~\ref{sec:cross_dataset}).
