\begin{abstract}
Automated semantic segmentation of cracks and spalling on concrete dam surfaces is essential for structural health monitoring, yet combining task-specific models with foundation models remains underexplored for infrastructure defects.
We propose \textbf{HeteroDistill}, a framework that augments a SegFormer-B2 backbone with \emph{Heterogeneous Dual-Teacher Knowledge Distillation} (DTKD), which transfers complementary knowledge from a task-specific teacher and a foundation-model teacher (SAM-LoRA) at zero additional inference cost.
On the \textbf{DamSegment} dataset \citep{damsegment2026} (1{,}500 images, 3~classes), HeteroDistill achieves 72.2--72.3\% foreground mIoU (depending on teacher-ensemble construction), with DTKD contributing +1.8--1.9 mIoU$_{\text{fg}}$ and the student surpassing the evaluated direct teacher ensemble by +1.5--1.6.
Multi-seed matched controls (5 conditions $\times$ 3 seeds) confirm that heterogeneous teachers produce a +1.5 mIoU$_{\text{fg}}$ gain with 5--7$\times$ variance reduction, while single-teacher KD provides no reliable benefit---supporting \emph{teacher diversity} as the main contributor under the evaluated settings.
We additionally investigate Dynamic Snake Convolution as a parallel crack-enhancement branch but find, through parameter-matched controls (DSConv vs.\ DCNv2 vs.\ standard convolution, 3 seeds each), that the geometric inductive bias provides no measurable benefit---a negative result relevant to the crack segmentation community.
Limitations and caveats are discussed in detail, including single-run SRL ablations and confounds in cross-dataset decomposition.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Concrete dams are safety-critical infrastructure whose structural integrity must be continuously monitored throughout multi-decade service lives.
Surface defects---predominantly cracks and spalling---serve as early indicators of internal deterioration, yet their manual inspection remains labour-intensive, subjective, and logistically constrained by the scale and accessibility of dam faces \citep{chen2024damsurvey,li2024dam}.
Automated semantic segmentation of these defects from high-resolution imagery therefore promises substantial gains in inspection efficiency and repeatability.

Recent deep-learning methods have advanced pixel-level defect segmentation considerably.
Encoder--decoder architectures such as U-Net \citep{ronneberger2015unet} and hierarchical transformers like SegFormer \citep{xie2021segformer} achieve competitive Intersection-over-Union (IoU) on benchmark crack datasets \citep{zou2019deepcrack,liu2019deepcrack}.
However, IoU and related region-overlap metrics are insensitive to topological errors: a prediction that fragments a continuous crack into disconnected segments may still score favourably on IoU while misrepresenting the structural extent of the defect \citep{pantoja2022topoloss}.
For structural safety assessment, the \emph{connectivity} of a predicted crack is as important as its pixel coverage---a fragmented prediction can lead an engineer to underestimate propagation length and thus misjudge residual capacity.

Topology-preserving losses such as clDice \citep{shit2021cldice} and persistent-homology penalties \citep{hu2019topology,clough2020topological} partially address this gap, yet they either incur prohibitive GPU memory for high-resolution dam images or operate on binary masks without multi-class support.
The recently proposed Skeleton Recall Loss (SRL) \citep{kirchhoff2024skeleton} overcomes both limitations by computing recall on CPU-extracted ground-truth skeletons with over 90\% reduction in computational overhead.
Concurrently, Dynamic Snake Convolution (DSConv) \citep{qi2023dynamic} reshapes kernel sampling grids to follow elongated, tortuous paths---a morphological prior that aligns naturally with the geometry of cracks.

Meanwhile, large vision foundation models such as SAM \citep{kirillov2023sam} and DINOv2 \citep{oquab2024dinov2} offer powerful pretrained representations, yet in our experiments, parameter-efficient fine-tuning via LoRA \citep{hu2022lora} on small industrial datasets underperforms fully fine-tuned task-specific architectures under the configurations we evaluated.
A natural question arises: \emph{can the complementary strengths of task-specific and foundation models be combined}?
We address this through knowledge distillation, where a student model learns from both a task-specific teacher and a foundation-model teacher.

In this paper we propose \textbf{HeteroDistill}, a framework that augments SegFormer-B2 with a parallel feature-extraction branch and dual-teacher knowledge distillation (DTKD) for complementary knowledge transfer. We investigate Dynamic Snake Convolution (DSConv) as the branch architecture but find, through parameter-matched controls, that the specific geometric bias provides no measurable advantage over standard or deformable convolutions. We additionally investigate Skeleton Recall Loss (SRL) and uncover an asymmetric role in distillation: SRL enriches teacher training but harms the student when combined with DTKD.
We evaluate on the semantic segmentation subset of the DamSegment dataset \citep{damsegment2026}, comprising 1{,}500 annotated dam surface images.
Our contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \emph{Heterogeneous dual-teacher knowledge distillation} (DTKD) that combines a task-specific teacher with a foundation-model teacher at zero inference overhead. DTKD adds +1.8--1.9 mIoU$_{\text{fg}}$ and the student surpasses the evaluated direct teacher ensemble by +1.5--1.6. Multi-seed matched controls (5 conditions $\times$ 3 seeds) confirm +1.5 mIoU$_{\text{fg}}$ net gain with 5--7$\times$ variance reduction, supporting teacher diversity as a key contributor (Section~\ref{sec:ablation}).
    \item A \emph{negative result on geometric inductive bias}: parameter-matched comparison of Dynamic Snake Convolution, DCNv2, and standard convolution (3 seeds each, ${\sim}$1.32M parameters) shows no measurable difference across segmentation and topology metrics (all mean $\pm$ std ranges overlap), indicating that snake-shaped sampling does not help crack segmentation on this dataset (Section~\ref{sec:ablation}). Limitations are discussed in Sections~\ref{sec:ablation}--\ref{sec:discussion}.
    \item A single-run observation that SRL may play an asymmetric role: SRL-trained teachers produce better students (+1.2 mIoU$_{\text{fg}}$), while adding SRL to student training under DTKD reduces performance ($-$1.1). Multi-seed confirmation is needed (Section~\ref{sec:srl}).
    \item A comprehensive evaluation comprising six baselines (including SAM-LoRA and DINOv2-LoRA), per-tier difficulty analysis, multi-seed stability, feature-cluster split sanity check, and zero-shot cross-dataset transfer (DamSegment$\to$S2DS). Under the evaluated configurations, task-specific design with knowledge distillation outperforms direct foundation-model fine-tuning.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Concrete defect segmentation}
\label{sec:related:defect}

Early CNN-based crack detectors such as CrackNet \citep{zhang2017cracknet} treated defect identification as pixel-level classification without pooling layers.
Fully convolutional designs subsequently enabled dense pixel-wise prediction: DeepCrack \citep{liu2019deepcrack} introduced deeply-supervised side outputs, while \citet{zou2019deepcrack} exploited hierarchical features with multi-scale fusion.

Transformer-based architectures have demonstrated improved global context modelling.
CrackFormer \citep{liu2021crackformer} embeds self-attention to capture long-range crack continuity, and SegFormer \citep{xie2021segformer} provides a lightweight hierarchical encoder with an all-MLP decoder.
Universal architectures such as Mask2Former \citep{cheng2022mask2former} further push segmentation accuracy on general benchmarks but have seen limited adoption for infrastructure defects due to annotation scarcity.

Applied to dam inspection specifically, \citet{arafin2024concrete} performed multi-class concrete defect segmentation using DeepLabV3+, while \citet{li2024dam} proposed a lightweight CNN-Transformer hybrid for dam crack patrol inspection.
\citet{chen2024damsurvey} surveyed visual perception methods for intelligent dam inspection, identifying limited dataset scale and poor topology preservation as key open challenges.
More recently, CCT~Net \citep{gao2026cctnet} combined CNN and Transformer branches for dam surface crack segmentation, achieving 88.7\% mIoU on a custom single-class dataset, though it does not address multi-class segmentation or topology preservation.

\subsection{Topology-aware segmentation}
\label{sec:related:topology}

\citet{hu2019topology} introduced a differentiable topological loss based on persistent homology.
\citet{clough2020topological} extended persistent-homology supervision to cardiac imaging.
However, persistent-homology computation scales poorly to high-resolution images.

For tubular structures, \citet{shit2021cldice} proposed clDice, a differentiable soft-skeleton metric, but it requires GPU-based skeletonisation with substantial memory overhead.
The Skeleton Recall Loss \citep{kirchhoff2024skeleton} pre-computes ground-truth skeletons on CPU and formulates a recall objective, achieving superior connectivity with over 90\% memory reduction relative to clDice.
Recent work has critically examined topology-aware losses: \citet{arora2025srlcritique} question whether SRL's benefits generalise beyond the original evaluation setting, and \citet{joo2026cts} provide a faithful implementation of the Crack Topology Score with extensions for handling common prediction artifacts.

Our work evaluates SRL in the multi-class dam defect setting; single-run ablations suggest an asymmetric pattern where SRL improves teacher utility for downstream distillation but harms student training when combined with soft-label objectives---consistent with the growing evidence that topology-aware losses require careful integration, though multi-seed validation is needed.

\subsection{Deformable and dynamic convolutions}
\label{sec:related:deformable}

Deformable Convolutional Networks (DCN) \citep{dai2017deformable} introduced learnable offsets for adaptive sampling.
Dynamic Snake Convolution (DSConv) \citep{qi2023dynamic} constrains offsets along a one-dimensional path, forcing snake-like sampling that traces elongated structures.
DSConv has since been adopted for various crack detection tasks: \citet{pan2025tunneldsconv} applied it to metro tunnel lining crack detection, and \citet{yu2024dscformer} proposed a dual-branch architecture combining enhanced DSConv with SegFormer for road crack segmentation, achieving competitive IoU on Crack3238 and FIND datasets.

Our work differs from \citet{yu2024dscformer} in three aspects: (1)~we address multi-class segmentation (crack \emph{and} spalling) rather than binary crack detection; (2)~we introduce dual-teacher knowledge distillation (DTKD) with optional agreement-aware pixel-level modulation, which is absent from their architecture-only approach; and (3)~we target dam-specific inspection with boundary and centreline evaluation (BF1, clDice, CompR) and cross-dataset transfer analysis.

\subsection{Knowledge distillation for segmentation}
\label{sec:related:kd}

Knowledge distillation (KD) \citep{hinton2015distilling} transfers knowledge from a teacher model to a smaller student by matching softened output distributions.
In semantic segmentation, structured KD approaches \citep{liu2019structured,wang2020intra} distil spatial relationships rather than just per-pixel logits.
Multi-teacher distillation \citep{you2017learning} combines knowledge from multiple specialised teachers, but typically uses uniform teacher weighting across all classes.
\citet{zhang2022camkd} proposed confidence-aware multi-teacher KD (CA-MKD) with sample-wise teacher reliability weighting, though at the sample level rather than per-class or per-pixel. Our agreement-aware variant operates at the pixel level, using inter-teacher KL divergence rather than individual teacher confidence, which better captures spatial ambiguity at defect boundaries.
Recently, \citet{wang2025taskspecifickd} demonstrated task-specific KD from vision foundation models for medical segmentation, showing that distilling SAM's knowledge into lightweight students outperforms direct fine-tuning---a finding consistent with our results in the infrastructure domain.

Our dual-teacher strategy differs by combining a task-specific teacher with a foundation-model teacher whose complementary strengths are empirically validated. Ablation shows that the dual-teacher ensemble itself is the key contributor, with per-class vs.\ equal weighting yielding negligible difference. Pixel-level agreement-aware modulation is explored as an extension.
This contrasts with CA-MKD's sample-level weighting and with \citet{wang2025taskspecifickd}'s single-teacher approach.

\subsection{Foundation models for segmentation}
\label{sec:related:foundation}

The Segment Anything Model (SAM) \citep{kirillov2023sam} and DINOv2 \citep{oquab2024dinov2} represent powerful vision foundation models.
Parameter-efficient fine-tuning via LoRA \citep{hu2022lora} enables adaptation with minimal trainable parameters (${\sim}5\%$).
While promising on natural images, their effectiveness on small-scale industrial datasets remains underexplored.
\citet{ye2024samdefect} applied SAM-based instance segmentation for structural damage detection, demonstrating SAM's potential for infrastructure inspection but also noting the need for domain-specific adaptation.
In our experiments, using foundation models as teachers in knowledge distillation outperforms direct LoRA fine-tuning under the configurations evaluated---a strategy that preserves inference efficiency while inheriting the foundation model's structural knowledge, though the comparison is limited by the absence of systematic hyperparameter tuning for the LoRA baselines.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Overview}

Fig.~\ref{fig:architecture} illustrates the HeteroDistill framework. Our approach enhances a SegFormer-B2 backbone at two levels: (1)~\emph{feature extraction} via a parallel branch (DSConv in our default configuration, though parameter-matched controls show the branch type does not significantly affect performance; Section~\ref{sec:ablation}), and (2)~\emph{knowledge transfer} via dual-teacher distillation (DTKD) to combine the strengths of task-specific and foundation models.

(a)~A SegFormer-B2 encoder with a parallel CrackSnake branch (DSConv) produces 3-class segmentation logits; the DSConv branch consumes encoder stages~2 and~3 (1-indexed; 0-indexed \texttt{hidden\_states[1]} at $H/8$ and \texttt{hidden\_states[2]} at $H/16$; see Section~\ref{sec:dsconv}) and additively enhances only the crack channel. (b)~Dual-teacher knowledge distillation (DTKD): Teacher~1 (DSConv+SRL, strong on pixel accuracy) and Teacher~2 (SAM-LoRA+SRL, strong on spalling component coverage recall) are combined via per-class probability-space weighting (see Section~\ref{sec:dtkd}; equal-weight logit averaging performs similarly). The dashed path shows an optional agreement-aware variant (Section~\ref{sec:ablation}) that modulates the KD loss by pixel-level teacher agreement; this is \emph{not} part of the default pipeline (row~c).}

\subsection{SegFormer-B2 backbone}

We adopt SegFormer-B2 \citep{xie2021segformer} as our backbone, providing a hierarchical Mix Transformer (MiT) encoder with four stages at resolutions $H/4$, $H/8$, $H/16$, and $H/32$ with channel dimensions 64, 128, 320, and 512. The all-MLP decoder fuses multi-scale features via linear projection and upsampling. We initialise from weights pretrained on ADE20K at $512{\times}512$.

\subsection{Parallel crack-enhancement branch}
\label{sec:dsconv}

We add a lightweight parallel branch (1.32M parameters, +4.8\% over SegFormer-B2) that produces a single-channel crack enhancement signal, which is added to the decoder's crack logit:
\begin{equation}
    \tilde{\mathbf{z}}_{\text{crack}} = \mathbf{z}_{\text{crack}} + \uparrow\!\mathbf{E}_{\text{crack}}
    \label{eq:dsconv}
\end{equation}
Spalling and background logits remain unmodified.

\paragraph{Architecture}
The branch fuses encoder stages~2 and~3 (at $H/8$ and $H/16$) via $1{\times}1$ projections to a hidden dimension $d{=}64$, followed by two parallel processing paths (one per spatial axis).
In our default configuration, each path uses a Dynamic Snake Convolution (DSConv) block \citep{qi2023dynamic} with $K{=}9$ sampling positions that performs deformable sampling along elongated structures via learned offsets with cumulative summation; the outputs are aggregated by a directional Conv2d and fused through a two-layer head (Conv2d $64{\to}32{\to}1$, zero-initialised so the branch starts as a no-op).

\paragraph{Branch type does not matter}
We initially hypothesised that DSConv's snake-shaped sampling would improve crack topology.
However, parameter-matched controls (Section~\ref{sec:ablation}, Table~\ref{tab:branch_compare}) comparing DSConv, Deformable Convolution v2 (DCNv2) \citep{dai2017deformable}, and standard convolution---all with ${\sim}$1.32M parameters and 3 seeds each---show no measurable difference across any segmentation or topology metric (all mean $\pm$ std ranges overlap).
The parallel branch provides useful additional capacity, but its geometric inductive bias does not matter on this dataset.
We retain DSConv in the default configuration but do not claim the branch type as a contribution.

\subsection{Dual-teacher knowledge distillation}
\label{sec:dtkd}

A key observation from our experiments is that different model architectures excel at different quality dimensions: the task-specific DSConv+SRL model (Teacher~1) achieves higher pixel-level accuracy (IoU), while the foundation-model SAM-LoRA (Teacher~2) achieves higher component recall (CompR$_{\text{fg}}$), driven primarily by its substantially stronger spalling component preservation (CompR$_{\text{sp}}$ 86.0\% vs.\ 73.4\%; Table~\ref{tab:foundation}). Standard single-teacher distillation cannot exploit this complementarity.

We propose \emph{dual-teacher knowledge distillation} (DTKD) that constructs an ensemble of teacher predictions:

\paragraph{Teacher models} We use two pretrained teachers, both trained with Skeleton Recall Loss (SRL) \citep{kirchhoff2024skeleton} to improve their utility as distillation teachers (the rationale for this choice is validated in Section~\ref{sec:ablation}). The two teachers differ in total training epochs and SRL activation schedule; full details are in Section~\ref{sec:implementation}.
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item \textbf{Teacher~1} ($T_1$): DSConv+SRL (task-specific, strong on pixel accuracy)
    \item \textbf{Teacher~2} ($T_2$): SAM-LoRA (foundation model, strong on spalling component coverage recall)
\end{itemize}
We select SAM-LoRA over DINOv2-LoRA as Teacher~2 because SAM-LoRA achieves substantially higher CompR$_{\text{fg}}$ (81.2\% vs.\ 76.5\%) and BF1$_{\text{fg}}$ (68.1\% vs.\ 62.4\%), providing complementary structural knowledge (Table~\ref{tab:main}). DINOv2-LoRA's advantage is limited to IoU$_{\text{sp}}$, which Teacher~1 already covers.

\paragraph{Teacher ensemble}
The two teacher predictions are combined by equal-weight logit averaging:
\begin{equation}
    \tilde{\mathbf{z}} = \tfrac{1}{2}\,(\mathbf{z}_{T_1} / \tau + \mathbf{z}_{T_2} / \tau), \qquad
    \tilde{\mathbf{p}} = \text{softmax}(\tilde{\mathbf{z}})
    \label{eq:dtkd_ensemble_logit}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{z}_{T_i}$ are the teacher logits and $\tau$ is the softmax temperature. This construction is parameter-free and treats both teachers symmetrically.

\paragraph{Per-class weighting variant}
We also explore a per-class probability-space variant:
\begin{equation}
    q_c = w_{1,c} \cdot p_{1,c} + w_{2,c} \cdot p_{2,c}, \qquad
    \tilde{p}_c = \frac{q_c}{\sum_{k} q_k}
    \label{eq:dtkd_ensemble_perclass}
\end{equation}
where $p_{i,c} = \text{softmax}(\mathbf{z}_{T_i} / \tau)_c$ and $w_{1,c} + w_{2,c} = 1$ for each class $c$. We set $w_{2,\text{cr}} = 0.6$, $w_{2,\text{sp}} = 0.3$, $w_{2,\text{bg}} = 0.5$ (chosen manually, no systematic search). These weights do not align with Teacher~2's actual strengths (its main advantage is spalling component recall, yet it receives only 30\% spalling weight). As shown in Table~\ref{tab:kd_weights}, the two constructions yield near-identical results (mIoU$_{\text{fg}}$ 72.2 vs.\ 72.3), confirming that DTKD's value derives from the dual-teacher ensemble itself, not the mixing procedure.

\paragraph{Distillation loss} The student is trained with:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{KD}} = \tau^2 \cdot \text{KL}\!\left(\tilde{\mathbf{p}} \,\|\, \text{softmax}(\mathbf{z}_s / \tau)\right)
    \label{eq:kd_loss}
\end{equation}
where $\tilde{\mathbf{p}}$ is the ensemble target (Eq.~\ref{eq:dtkd_ensemble_logit} by default; Eq.~\ref{eq:dtkd_ensemble_perclass} yields near-identical results) and $\mathbf{z}_s$ the student logits.

\paragraph{Agreement-aware distillation}
When two teachers with different architectures and training recipes are combined, their predictions inevitably disagree at certain pixels---particularly at ambiguous crack--background boundaries and thin structures. At such pixels the ensemble soft label is unreliable, and forcing the student to match it may introduce noise. We note that high inter-teacher agreement does not guarantee correctness---both teachers may agree on an incorrect label---but empirically, agreement-weighted pixels yield more consistent supervision than disagreement regions.
We quantify per-pixel teacher agreement using the KL divergence between the two softened teacher distributions:
\begin{equation}
    d(i) = \text{KL}\!\left(\text{softmax}(\mathbf{z}_{T_1}^{(i)} / \tau) \,\|\, \text{softmax}(\mathbf{z}_{T_2}^{(i)} / \tau)\right)
    \label{eq:teacher_disagree}
\end{equation}
where $i$ indexes spatial positions. An agreement map is derived as $a(i) = 1 - d(i) / \max_j d(j)$, ranging from 1 (full agreement) to 0 (maximum disagreement). The agreement-aware distillation loss modulates the per-pixel KD contribution:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{KD}}^{\text{conf}} = \tau^2 \cdot \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i} a(i) \cdot \text{KL}\!\left(\tilde{\mathbf{p}}^{(i)} \,\|\, \text{softmax}(\mathbf{z}_s^{(i)} / \tau)\right)
    \label{eq:conf_kd}
\end{equation}
The intent is that the student learns strongly from the ensemble where both teachers concur, but falls back on the ground-truth supervised loss at ambiguous pixels. The agreement map is computed within the existing forward pass (no additional inference cost).

\textbf{Implicit sample-level effect.}
Because the loss is normalised by pixel count $N$ (not by $\sum_i a(i)$), an image with low overall agreement has lower total KD loss magnitude than one with high agreement. This means the formulation does \emph{not} operate purely at the pixel level: it also implicitly attenuates the KD loss for high-disagreement \emph{samples}. The sample-level reweighting ablation (Section~\ref{sec:ablation}) therefore does not fully isolate pixel-level from sample-level effects---both mechanisms are entangled in Eq.~\ref{eq:conf_kd}.

\textbf{Technical limitations.}
The per-image max-normalisation $a(i) = 1 - d(i)/\max_j d(j)$ has several issues: (i)~division by zero when $\max_j d(j) = 0$ (both teachers agree everywhere; in practice this is handled by adding $\epsilon = 10^{-8}$ in the implementation); (ii)~the agreement map is scale-set by a single pixel (the most disagreeing one), making it sensitive to outliers; (iii)~KL divergence is asymmetric ($\text{KL}(T_1 \| T_2) \neq \text{KL}(T_2 \| T_1)$); (iv)~per-image normalisation makes the agreement values incomparable across samples.
Alternative designs (e.g., JS divergence, sum-normalisation $\sum_i a(i) \cdot \text{KL}_i / \sum_i a(i)$ to remove the implicit sample attenuation, exponential decay, or fixed-threshold gating) may address these issues but are left to future work.
Fig.~\ref{fig:agreement} visualises the disagreement map on representative Hard-tier samples: high-disagreement pixels (bright regions) concentrate at crack--background boundaries and thin branching points, precisely where the ensemble soft label is least reliable.
We empirically validate that teacher disagreement correlates with sample difficulty: the mean per-sample KL divergence between teachers is 43\% higher on Hard-tier images than on Easy-tier images (Section~\ref{sec:ablation}).

\paragraph{Total loss} The primary training objective (row~c) balances the segmentation loss and distillation loss:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L} = (1-\alpha) \left[\lambda_1 \mathcal{L}_{\text{CE}} + \lambda_2 \mathcal{L}_{\text{Dice}}\right] + \alpha \, \mathcal{L}_{\text{KD}}
    \label{eq:total_loss}
\end{equation}
with $\alpha = 0.5$, $\tau = 4$, and $\lambda_1 = \lambda_2 = 0.5$.
The agreement-aware variant (row~g) substitutes $\mathcal{L}_{\text{KD}}^{\text{conf}}$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:conf_kd}) for $\mathcal{L}_{\text{KD}}$, down-weighting unreliable pixels.
Note that SRL is used only during \emph{teacher} training; the student does not use SRL, as we find it conflicts with the distillation objective (Section~\ref{sec:ablation}).

\subsection{Test-time augmentation}

At inference, we optionally employ multi-scale TTA: each image is evaluated at three scales (0.75$\times$, 1.0$\times$, 1.25$\times$) with horizontal and vertical flips (9 views). Logits are averaged before argmax.

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Dataset}

We evaluate on the \textbf{semantic segmentation subset} of the DamSegment dataset \citep{damsegment2026}, which comprises 3{,}500 image patches captured during an on-site visit of a concrete dam, supporting classification, detection, and segmentation tasks.
The segmentation subset contains 1{,}500 pixel-level annotated images with three classes (background, crack, spalling) at $512{\times}512$ resolution, organised into three difficulty tiers (Easy, Medium, Hard; 500 each) based on crack detection difficulty, enabling curriculum learning.
We split the data into train/val/test sets of 1{,}200/150/150 images, stratified by tier. Splitting is at the patch level; patches from adjacent regions may share visual characteristics (see Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).
Fig.~\ref{fig:dataset} shows representative samples from each tier with ground-truth overlays; crack pixels range from 0.3\% to 7.9\% and spalling from 1.0\% to 11.7\%, illustrating the extreme class imbalance.

\subsection{Implementation details}
\label{sec:implementation}

All models are trained on a single NVIDIA A40 GPU using PyTorch.
Table~\ref{tab:training_config} summarises training configurations for all models.
All models use AdamW optimiser with weight decay $10^{-4}$ and cosine annealing LR schedule (with 5-epoch linear warmup where noted).
The segmentation loss is 0.5\,CE + 0.5\,Dice for all models except Mask2Former (which uses its native set-prediction loss).
Data augmentation consists of random horizontal and vertical flips ($p{=}0.5$ each) and random brightness/contrast ($p{=}0.3$); images are normalised with ImageNet statistics.
The DSConv branch uses $d{=}64$, $K{=}9$.

\subsection{Evaluation metrics}

We report complementary metrics capturing different aspects of segmentation quality:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item \textbf{mIoU$_{\text{fg}}$}: Mean IoU of crack and spalling classes.
    \item \textbf{IoU$_{\text{cr}}$}, \textbf{IoU$_{\text{sp}}$}: Per-class IoU.
    \item \textbf{BF1}: Boundary F1 score (2-pixel tolerance) \citep{csurka2013good}.
    \item \textbf{clDice}: Centreline Dice via skeletonisation \citep{shit2021cldice}.
    \item \textbf{CompR}: Component Recall---fraction of ground-truth connected components for which $\geq$50\% of pixels are correctly predicted (i.e., the predicted class mask overlaps the GT component by at least half). This is a \emph{coverage recall} metric, not a connectivity metric: a GT crack component fragmented into multiple disconnected prediction segments still counts as ``recalled'' provided the fragments collectively cover $\geq$50\% of the GT pixels. CompR does not penalise false-positive components, over-prediction, or component merging/splitting. A model predicting all pixels as a given class would achieve CompR $\approx$ 100\% for that class. CompR should always be interpreted alongside precision-sensitive metrics (BF1, IoU) and connectivity-sensitive metrics (clDice). The 50\% threshold is a single operating point; we did not perform sensitivity analysis across thresholds (e.g., 25\%, 75\%).
    \item \textbf{CompP}: Component Precision---fraction of \emph{predicted} connected components for which $\geq$50\% of pixels overlap with GT pixels of the same class. CompP penalises spurious predicted components that do not correspond to any real defect.
    \item \textbf{CompF1}: Harmonic mean of CompR and CompP, providing a single component-level quality score that balances recall of GT components with precision of predicted components.
    \item \textbf{PathCont}: Path Continuity---for each GT connected component, we extract its morphological skeleton and identify skeleton endpoints (pixels with exactly one 8-connected skeleton neighbour). A component is ``testable'' if its skeleton has $\geq$2 endpoints. PathCont is the fraction of testable GT components whose skeleton endpoints are mutually reachable through the predicted mask (i.e., all endpoints fall within the same connected component of the prediction restricted to the GT component's spatial extent). PathCont captures whether the prediction preserves the end-to-end connectivity of elongated structures; it is undefined (excluded from averaging) for images where no GT component has $\geq$2 skeleton endpoints.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{(a)$\to$(b): Adding a parallel branch}
Adding the DSConv branch (row~b) produces minimal mIoU change (+0.1) but improves boundary quality: BF1$_{\text{cr}}$ +1.0, BF1$_{\text{fg}}$ +2.0.

\paragraph{Parameter-matched branch comparison}
To isolate the contribution of DSConv's geometric inductive bias, we compare three branch types---all sharing identical stage projections, output head, and ${\sim}$1.32M parameters---trained with 3 independent seeds each (Table~\ref{tab:branch_compare}):
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item \textbf{DSConv}: Dynamic Snake Convolution with snake-shaped sampling (1.32M params);
    \item \textbf{DCNv2}: Deformable Convolution v2 \citep{dai2017deformable} with learned general offsets (1.33M params);
    \item \textbf{StdConv}: Standard convolution with no deformable sampling (1.32M params).

This null result has an important implication: DTKD, not branch geometry, is the primary contributor to HeteroDistill's performance. We retain DSConv in the default pipeline as a reasonable architectural choice but do not claim it as a contribution.

\paragraph{(b$_1$)--(b$_2$) vs.\ (c): Single- vs.\ dual-teacher KD}
To verify that both teachers are necessary, we train single-teacher KD variants with identical student settings as row~(c): row~(b$_1$) uses only Teacher~1 (task-specific) and row~(b$_2$) uses only Teacher~2 (foundation model), with the other teacher's weight set to zero.
Neither single teacher matches the dual-teacher result: T1-only achieves 69.0\% mIoU$_{\text{fg}}$ and T2-only 69.2\%, both ${\sim}$3 points below dual-teacher DTKD (72.3\%) and even below the no-KD baseline (row~b, 70.4\%).
This indicates that a single teacher's soft labels, occupying 50\% of the training signal ($\alpha{=}0.5$), can introduce more noise than useful information when the teacher lacks complementary knowledge to counterbalance its biases.
The two teachers nonetheless show complementary strengths: T2-only achieves substantially higher CompR$_{\text{fg}}$ (85.0 vs.\ T1-only's 81.0), reflecting SAM-LoRA's stronger component preservation, while both show similar IoU$_{\text{cr}}$ (${\sim}$56).
Dual-teacher KD combines these complementary strengths, achieving the best mIoU$_{\text{fg}}$ and IoU$_{\text{sp}}$ while maintaining strong crack metrics.

\paragraph{(b)$\to$(c): Adding DTKD}
DTKD provides substantial pixel-level improvement: mIoU$_{\text{fg}}$ +1.9, IoU$_{\text{cr}}$ +1.1, IoU$_{\text{sp}}$ +2.8, clDice$_{\text{cr}}$ +1.5, and BF1$_{\text{cr}}$ +0.6, while also improving component recall: CompR$_{\text{fg}}$ +2.6 (80.4$\to$83.0).
CompR$_{\text{cr}}$ decreases modestly from 83.8 to 82.1 ($-$1.7), as DTKD's soft-label supervision slightly smooths fine crack structures, but this is more than offset by substantially improved spalling component recall.
The dual-teacher ensemble ensures that the student receives complementary guidance from both teacher architectures (Section~\ref{sec:dtkd}).

\paragraph{(c)$\to$(d): Test-time augmentation}
TTA provides modest additional gains (mIoU$_{\text{fg}}$ +0.4, IoU$_{\text{cr}}$ +0.8) but reduces component recall (CompR$_{\text{fg}}$ 83.0$\to$80.2), suggesting that multi-scale/flip aggregation improves pixel coverage but may smooth fine structural details.

\paragraph{(c)$\to$(g): Agreement-aware KD (exploratory)}
Agreement-aware distillation (Eq.~\ref{eq:conf_kd}) improves boundary quality in a single run: BF1$_{\text{cr}}$ +0.8, clDice$_{\text{cr}}$ +0.6, mIoU$_{\text{fg}}$ +0.2 (within seed noise of 0.52). CompR$_{\text{cr}}$ decreases by $-$0.7. Multi-seed validation is needed.

An ablation using \emph{explicit} sample-level reweighting (without pixel-level modulation) degraded performance (mIoU$_{\text{fg}}$ 72.1, BF1$_{\text{fg}}$ 70.0), suggesting pixel-level modulation is the active mechanism. However, the agreement-aware KD loss contains implicit sample-level attenuation, so the two are not fully disentangled.
Teacher disagreement correlates with difficulty (+43\% KL divergence on Hard vs.\ Easy), but this does not translate into effective sample-level curriculum learning---consistent with findings that reweighting strategies underperform on small datasets.

\paragraph{Effect of SRL (rows e--f)}
\label{sec:srl}
Skeleton Recall Loss (SRL) \citep{kirchhoff2024skeleton} penalises low predicted probability at ground-truth skeleton pixels $\mathcal{S}$, precomputed offline via morphological skeletonisation (\texttt{skimage.morphology.skeletonize}, default Zhang method):
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{SRL}} = 1 - \frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}|} \sum_{s \in \mathcal{S}} p_{\text{crack}}(s)
    \label{eq:srl}
\end{equation}
where $p_{\text{crack}}(s) = \text{softmax}(\mathbf{z})_{\text{crack}}$ is the predicted crack probability at skeleton pixel $s$. When the ground-truth mask contains no crack pixels ($|\mathcal{S}|{=}0$), the SRL term is set to zero for that sample.
We apply SRL only to the crack class (weight $\lambda_3 = 0.05$). For the 100-epoch branch-augmented SegFormer models in this table, SRL is activated at epoch 60; for the 50-epoch SAM-LoRA teacher, SRL is activated at epoch 20 (see Section~\ref{sec:implementation}).
Rows (e)--(f) show the effect of adding SRL to the pipeline.
Adding SRL to the branch model (row e) provides no crack improvement (IoU$_{\text{cr}}$ $-$0.1, BF1$_{\text{cr}}$ $-$0.3) and reduces component recall: CompR$_{\text{fg}}$ drops from 80.4 to 77.8 ($-$2.6), driven by CompR$_{\text{sp}}$ falling from 77.0 to 73.4 as SRL's crack-focused supervision fragments spalling components at ambiguous boundaries.
Adding DTKD on top of SRL (row f) partially recovers component coverage (CompR$_{\text{fg}}$ 77.8$\to$80.6) but the resulting model (f, mIoU$_{\text{fg}}$ 71.2\%) underperforms the SRL-free pipeline (c, mIoU$_{\text{fg}}$ 72.3\%) by \textbf{$-$1.1}.
This suggests an optimisation conflict between the SRL and KD objectives: the student learns better from teacher signals alone without the competing skeleton objective.
Multi-seed experiments (Table~\ref{tab:multiseed}) further confirm that the SRL path is unstable (mIoU$_{\text{fg}}$ std 2.42 vs.\ 0.70 for branch-only and 0.52 for DTKD).

\paragraph{Overall progression}
From baseline (a) to the primary method HeteroDistill (c) within this table, the cumulative improvement is +2.0 mIoU$_{\text{fg}}$, +2.0 BF1$_{\text{fg}}$, +3.7 clDice$_{\text{fg}}$, and +6.0 CompR$_{\text{fg}}$ (the +2.0 vs.\ +1.9 in Table~\ref{tab:main} reflects the different baseline seed). DSConv contributes boundary quality and spalling skeleton coverage; DTKD contributes pixel accuracy and crack-specific clDice$_{\text{cr}}$ (+1.5, from 81.8 to 83.3) on this split (see Table~\ref{tab:group_split} for an exploratory cluster-split comparison). The exploratory agreement-aware extension (g) adds modest boundary gains (+0.8 BF1$_{\text{cr}}$) that require multi-seed confirmation.

\paragraph{Statistical significance}
Paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests (DSConv-only vs.\ HeteroDistill, 150 test images) show seven of ten per-class metrics reaching \emph{uncorrected} significance at $\alpha{=}0.05$: six favour DTKD---IoU$_{\text{cr}}$ ($p{=}0.008$), Dice$_{\text{cr}}$ ($p{=}0.007$), BF1$_{\text{cr}}$ ($p{=}0.046$), clDice$_{\text{cr}}$ ($p{<}0.001$), IoU$_{\text{sp}}$ ($p{=}0.016$), Dice$_{\text{sp}}$ ($p{=}0.016$)---while CompR$_{\text{cr}}$ decreases ($p{=}0.040$). After Holm--Bonferroni correction for 10 comparisons, only clDice$_{\text{cr}}$ ($p_{\text{adj}}{=}0.010$) survives; directional consistency is encouraging but per-class IoU gains are not confirmed after correction. Spalling sample sizes are small ($n{=}31$--40). Tier-stratified analysis shows the strongest effects on Hard images (IoU$_{\text{cr}}$ $p{=}0.003$, clDice$_{\text{cr}}$ $p{<}0.001$, uncorrected).

\paragraph{Multi-seed training stability}
To assess robustness to random initialisation, we train four key configurations---SegFormer-B2 (baseline), branch-only (row~b), branch+SRL, and HeteroDistill (row~c, final model)---with three independent seeds (42, 123, 2024) and report mean $\pm$ standard deviation in Table~\ref{tab:multiseed}.

\textbf{Caveat:} HeteroDistill seeds fix both teacher checkpoints and vary only the student seed. \subsection{Loss function and training strategy exploration}
\label{sec:strategy}

\paragraph{Error analysis}
To guide loss function design, we analyse the confusion matrix of the DTKD model (row~c).
For crack pixels, precision is 68.8\% while recall is 79.7\%, yielding a false-positive to false-negative ratio of 1.78:1.
The majority of false positives occur at crack--background boundaries, where thin crack predictions leak into adjacent background pixels.
This precision bottleneck motivates exploring loss functions that penalise false positives more heavily.

\paragraph{Strategy variants}
Table~\ref{tab:strategy} summarises six training strategies applied on top of the DTKD pipeline (row~c), targeting crack precision improvement through loss modification or sampling adjustment.
All variants use the same branch-augmented SegFormer architecture with DTKD; they differ only in the segmentation loss or sampling strategy.
Tversky loss \citep{salehi2017tversky} generalises Dice by introducing asymmetric FP/FN weights ($\alpha{>}0.5$ penalises false positives more heavily); WABL (Width-Aware Boundary Loss) applies an adaptive CE penalty at thin-crack boundary pixels where width falls below a threshold; Boundary Dice restricts the Dice computation to a dilated boundary region around ground-truth contours.

\paragraph{Crack--spalling tradeoff}
A consistent pattern emerges across all loss-based strategies: improving crack boundary precision degrades spalling metrics.
Tversky loss with $\alpha{=}0.6$ (stronger FP penalty) improves BF1$_{\text{sp}}$ by +3.4 over the baseline, but reduces mIoU$_{\text{fg}}$ by $-$0.6 and CompR$_{\text{fg}}$ by $-$3.6.
WABL, which specifically targets thin-crack boundary false positives, causes the largest spalling regression (IoU$_{\text{sp}}$ $-$1.4, BF1$_{\text{sp}}$ $-$1.6).
Combining WABL with Tversky amplifies the degradation (IoU$_{\text{sp}}$ $-$2.8).
This tradeoff may arise partly from shared geometric boundaries where crack and spalling co-occur; loss terms that suppress crack false positives may inadvertently penalise adjacent spalling predictions.

\paragraph{Hard-tier fine-tuning}
Sampling-based approaches partially mitigate this tradeoff.
Fine-tuning from the DTKD checkpoint with 70\% Hard-tier oversampling and WABL achieves BF1$_{\text{fg}}$ 72.2 (comparable to the best loss-based variant) while maintaining better spalling coverage (IoU$_{\text{sp}}$ 85.0 vs.\ 83.4--85.4 for loss variants) and the highest clDice$_{\text{fg}}$ (87.3).
However, no strategy surpasses the base DTKD model on mIoU$_{\text{fg}}$ (72.3), indicating that the standard CE+Dice combination is already near-optimal for this dataset and that the crack precision bottleneck requires fundamentally different approaches (e.g., higher-resolution inputs or explicit boundary modelling in the architecture).

\textit{Branch$\times$DTKD interaction.}
The factorial analysis reveals an interaction between the parallel branch and DTKD. DTKD improves mIoU$_{\text{fg}}$ by +1.9 when combined with the branch but \emph{degrades} performance by $-$0.7 on the plain SegFormer backbone. Since the branch type itself does not matter (Table~\ref{tab:branch_compare}), this interaction likely reflects \emph{architectural affinity} with Teacher~1 (which also has a parallel branch), rather than DSConv's specific geometric bias. Without a matching branch, the student cannot effectively leverage Teacher~1's distillation signal.

\textit{Student surpasses teacher ensemble.}
The DTKD student (72.3\%) surpasses the evaluated direct teacher ensemble (70.7\%, equal logit averaging) by +1.6 mIoU$_{\text{fg}}$, indicating that the student benefits from more than just the averaged teacher signal. However, this comparison involves different training histories and should not be interpreted as a clean ablation of distillation vs.\ ensembling.

\textit{Teacher diversity controls.}
In matched multi-seed experiments (3 seeds per condition, same T1 checkpoint per seed, same $\alpha$, $\tau$), heterogeneous teachers produce a clear net gain: Hetero equal-wt achieves 71.7\,$\pm$\,0.1 and Hetero class-cond achieves 71.9\,$\pm$\,0.2, both substantially above the No-KD baseline (70.3\,$\pm$\,1.0, $+$1.5). T1-only KD (69.7\,$\pm$\,1.5) and duplicated T1 (70.1\,$\pm$\,1.3) fall within the noise of the No-KD baseline, confirming that single-teacher KD provides no reliable benefit. Using two same-architecture teachers with different seeds (70.4\%, single run) matches the DSConv-only baseline, suggesting that seed diversity alone provides no benefit.

\textit{Isolation of net gain.}
Multi-seed matched controls confirm that heterogeneous KD produces a robust net gain over the No-KD baseline: +1.5 mIoU$_{\text{fg}}$ (71.7--71.9 vs.\ 70.3). Notably, heterogeneous teachers also achieve 5--7$\times$ lower variance (std 0.1--0.2 vs.\ 1.0), indicating that dual-teacher distillation provides strong regularisation. A small residual gap remains between the matched heterogeneous mean (71.7--71.9) and the full DTKD model (72.3, single seed) of ${\sim}$0.4--0.6, attributable to seed variation rather than a systematic mechanism difference.

\textit{Not soft-target regularisation.}
Label smoothing (68.7\%) underperforms the branch-only baseline (70.4\%) by $-$1.7, ruling out generic soft-target regularisation as the mechanism behind DTKD's improvement. This is consistent with \citet{yuan2020labelsmooth}'s finding that label smoothing acts as a form of self-training regularisation rather than knowledge transfer; the benefit of DTKD derives from actual teacher knowledge, not from softer training signals per se.

\subsection{Cross-dataset evaluation}
\label{sec:cross_dataset}

To assess generalisability beyond the training domain, we perform zero-shot transfer evaluation on the S2DS dataset \citep{s2ds2021}, which contains 743 images ($1024{\times}1024$) of concrete surfaces with pixel-level annotations for eight classes: background, crack, spalling, corrosion, efflorescence, vegetation, exposed reinforcement, and control point.
We map S2DS crack and spalling to our label scheme and treat all other classes as background, since they fall outside the DamSegment label space.
All images are resized to $512{\times}512$ (bilinear for images, nearest-neighbour for masks); predictions are evaluated at this downsampled resolution. The $2{\times}$ downsampling may delete single-pixel-wide crack annotations, potentially inflating apparent crack IoU; we did not measure crack pixel retention before and after resizing. A more robust protocol would upsample predictions to the original $1024{\times}1024$ resolution before evaluation.
The S2DS test split contains 93~images, of which 27 contain only crack, 20 contain only spalling, and 46 are background-only; crack pixels account for 0.12\% and spalling for 4.27\% of the test area.
No fine-tuning is performed: models trained on DamSegment are applied directly.

\textbf{Open-set rejection.} S2DS contains five defect classes absent from DamSegment (corrosion, efflorescence, vegetation, exposed reinforcement, control point), all mapped to background. The evaluation therefore conflates domain shift with open-set rejection: models must not only recognise crack and spalling in a new visual domain, but also correctly reject defect classes never seen during training. The reported metrics do not separate these two sources of error.

\textbf{Caveats.}
This decomposition is \emph{not} a clean ablation:
(i)~HeteroDistill simultaneously adds DTKD and removes student SRL, so the +1.6 over branch+SRL conflates both changes;
(ii)~SegFormer-B2's near-zero transfer likely reflects class collapse, inflating all subsequent gaps;
(iii)~the branch modifies only the crack channel, yet the largest gains appear in spalling IoU, suggesting indirect mechanisms;
(iv)~all results are single-seed and conflate domain shift with open-set rejection of five unseen classes (see above), and the $2{\times}$ downsampling may delete fine crack annotations.
The branch-equipped architecture is \emph{associated with} better cross-domain transfer, but individual component contributions cannot be isolated. Since branch type does not affect in-domain metrics (Table~\ref{tab:branch_compare}), the cross-dataset advantage likely stems from additional capacity rather than geometric bias.
TTA does not improve cross-dataset performance (18.7\%$\to$18.6\%).

The branch+SRL teacher and HeteroDistill localise defect regions despite the domain shift but exhibit visible over-prediction, particularly at region boundaries---consistent with the limited mIoU$_{\text{fg}}$ (${\leq}$18.7\%) reported above.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{DTKD: benefits and limitations}
On the original split, DTKD adds +1.9 mIoU$_{\text{fg}}$ over the branch-only baseline, though multi-seed means narrow this to +1.0 (70.8\% vs.\ 71.8\%, Table~\ref{tab:multiseed}). Matched multi-seed controls confirm a robust +1.5 gain with 5--7$\times$ variance reduction (Table~\ref{tab:factorial}). A $2{\times}2$ factorial analysis reveals an interaction: DTKD helps only with the parallel branch (+1.9) but degrades the plain SegFormer ($-$0.7), likely due to architectural affinity with Teacher~1 rather than branch geometry (Table~\ref{tab:branch_compare}). The student surpasses the evaluated direct teacher ensemble by +1.5--1.6 (Table~\ref{tab:factorial}). Cross-seed stability improves modestly (std 0.70$\to$0.52, three seeds). The gain does not replicate on the cluster split ($-$0.6, Table~\ref{tab:group_split}), though this is confounded by halved training data and different test composition. On S2DS, HeteroDistill's +1.6 over the branch+SRL teacher conflates DTKD with the removal of student SRL (Section~\ref{sec:cross_dataset}).

\paragraph{DTKD hyperparameter sensitivity check}
Table~\ref{tab:hyper} reports a star search over temperature $\tau \in \{2, 4, 6\}$ and KD weight $\alpha \in \{0.3, 0.5, 0.7\}$ (single run per setting, different seeds). Excluding $\tau{=}2$ ($-$1.4\%, consistent with overly sharp teacher signals), all settings fall within 0.9\% of the default---well within seed noise (std $\approx$ 0.5, Table~\ref{tab:multiseed}). We retain $\tau{=}4$, $\alpha{=}0.5$ without claiming optimality.

\paragraph{Granularity of difficulty-aware modulation}
Inter-teacher disagreement provides a free quality signal that correlates with sample difficulty (+43\% KL divergence on Hard vs.\ Easy). Leveraging this signal at the \emph{pixel level} (agreement-aware KD) shows promising single-run results on boundary metrics, while using it for \emph{sample-level} curriculum learning degrades performance. This suggests that on small datasets, every sample is needed for generalisation, but noisy pixels within a sample may be safely down-weighted---though multi-seed validation is needed to confirm that the pixel-level gains exceed random variation.

\paragraph{SRL: useful for teachers, harmful for students}
In a single teacher-swap ablation, the SRL-trained teacher produces a better student (+1.2 mIoU$_{\text{fg}}$) despite SRL degrading the teacher's own component coverage. As a student training component, SRL appears to interfere with DTKD's soft-label supervision ($-$1.1 mIoU$_{\text{fg}}$, single run). If confirmed by multi-seed experiments, this asymmetry---that optimal teacher and student training recipes differ---would have broader implications for distillation pipeline design. The current evidence is suggestive but not conclusive.

\paragraph{Indirect use of foundation models}
Under the evaluated LoRA configurations, directly fine-tuning SAM or DINOv2 underperforms task-specific architectures by 2--4\% mIoU$_{\text{fg}}$. Using them as \emph{teachers} in knowledge distillation yields better results in our experiments, though this comparison is limited by the absence of hyperparameter tuning for the foundation model baselines (see Section~\ref{sec:experiments}).
The ensemble analysis (Section~\ref{sec:ensemble}) confirms that cross-architecture models produce complementary error patterns: G0+SAM2 ensemble yields 5$\times$ the gain of same-architecture multi-seed ensemble (+0.56 vs.\ +0.10 mIoU$_{\text{fg}}$). This complementarity is a necessary condition for DTKD to be effective, and multi-seed matched controls confirm this complementarity translates to a robust net gain (+1.5 mIoU$_{\text{fg}}$, Section~\ref{sec:ablation}).

\paragraph{Crack--spalling tradeoff}
The persistent tradeoff across all loss-based strategies (Section~\ref{sec:strategy}) arises from shared geometric boundaries between defect types. Pixel-level loss modification alone cannot resolve the crack precision bottleneck without collateral damage to spalling, suggesting that sampling-based, architectural, or post-processing approaches (e.g., class-specific CRF refinement) are more promising.

\paragraph{Practical implications}
The student has identical inference cost to the branch-augmented SegFormer (51 FPS, 28.7M parameters), with teachers used only during training. DTKD improves all difficulty tiers, with the largest gain on Easy (+3.4 mIoU$_{\text{fg}}$).

\paragraph{Failure modes and limitations}
The model underperforms on (i)~thin hairline cracks (${<}$2 pixels wide) and (ii)~co-occurring crack--spalling regions where it over-predicts one class at the expense of the other.
DamSegment comprises images from a single dam site with patch-level splitting; spatially adjacent patches may share visual characteristics. To partially assess near-duplicate leakage, we constructed a \emph{feature-cluster split} (ResNet-50 features, agglomerative clustering, 100 groups) after all design decisions were locked. All models were retrained from prescribed pretrained initialisations on this split.

\paragraph{Caveats on the cluster split}
The cluster split differs substantially from the original in size and composition: 631/369/500 (train/val/test) with imbalanced tiers (21/41/38\% Easy/Medium/Hard), vs.\ the original 1{,}200/150/150 with balanced tiers. Five of six models score \emph{higher} on the cluster split despite the smaller training set; the reason is unknown and we did not compute per-split diagnostics (pixel prevalence, component statistics, feature similarity). The two test sets are therefore \emph{not directly comparable}.

Models do not collapse under this split ($\pm$3 points), but DTKD's ranking advantage is not preserved: the branch-only model (73.3), branch+SRL (73.1), and SegFormer-B2 (73.0) all surpass HeteroDistill (72.7). This reversal is confounded by the halved training set and different test composition, so it cannot be attributed solely to leakage correction—nor can it rule out leakage on the original split.

This split addresses only \emph{visual} near-duplicates; patch coordinates were unavailable, so spatial leakage remains possible. A definitive assessment would require coordinate-based grouping, matched split ratios, and per-split diagnostics. This experiment is a \emph{partial sanity check}, not a leakage proof.

\paragraph{Test-set exposure during development}
Test-set metrics were observed during development across ablation rows; small incremental gains (mIoU$_{\text{fg}}$ +0.1--0.2) should be interpreted as upper bounds. All decisions were finalised before the cluster split was constructed. The multi-seed analysis (Table~\ref{tab:multiseed}) suggests DTKD's contribution is more modest than the single-seed +1.9: mean mIoU$_{\text{fg}}$ differs by only 1.0 point (branch-only 70.8\% vs.\ HeteroDistill 71.8\%, std 0.70 vs.\ 0.52). A fully rigorous protocol would require locking decisions on the validation set alone; this limitation is common in fixed-benchmark studies.
Crack IoU remains challenging (${\sim}$59\%) due to extreme class imbalance.

\paragraph{Branch geometry: a null result}
Parameter-matched controls (Table~\ref{tab:branch_compare}) show that DSConv's snake-shaped sampling provides no measurable advantage over DCNv2 or standard convolution across segmentation metrics (mIoU$_{\text{fg}}$, BF1$_{\text{cr}}$, clDice$_{\text{cr}}$) and topology metrics (CompR$_{\text{cr}}$, CompF1$_{\text{cr}}$, PathCont$_{\text{cr}}$). All three branches produce indistinguishable results (overlapping mean $\pm$ std ranges) with ${\sim}$1.32M parameters and 3 seeds. This null result suggests that the parallel branch provides useful additional capacity for crack feature extraction, but the specific geometric inductive bias (snake-shaped vs.\ general deformable vs.\ standard) does not matter on this dataset. Possible explanations include: (i)~DamSegment's cracks may not be sufficiently elongated to benefit from directional sampling; (ii)~the SegFormer backbone already captures sufficient multi-scale context; or (iii)~1{,}200 training images may be insufficient for the deformable sampling to learn meaningful crack-specific offsets. This finding strengthens the case for DTKD as the primary contribution.

\paragraph{Topology evaluation}
We report component-level topology metrics (CompF1, PathCont) in addition to clDice and CompR. The parameter-matched branch comparison (Table~\ref{tab:branch_compare}) includes these metrics and confirms the null result across all topology measures. The CompR threshold (50\%) is a single operating point. True connectivity metrics such as the Crack Topology Score \citep{joo2026cts} were not computed.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We presented HeteroDistill, a segmentation framework for concrete dam defects that combines a parallel feature-extraction branch with dual-teacher knowledge distillation (DTKD).
On DamSegment, HeteroDistill achieves 72.2--72.3\% mIoU$_{\text{fg}}$ at 51 FPS with only 1.32M parameter overhead and no additional inference cost from distillation.
DTKD is the primary contributor, adding +1.8--1.9 mIoU$_{\text{fg}}$; multi-seed matched controls confirm +1.5 mIoU$_{\text{fg}}$ net gain and 5--7$\times$ variance reduction from teacher diversity (Section~\ref{sec:ablation}).
A parameter-matched branch comparison (DSConv vs.\ DCNv2 vs.\ standard Conv, 3 seeds each) shows no measurable difference (all mean $\pm$ std ranges overlap), establishing that the branch's geometric inductive bias does not matter on this dataset.
Limitations discussed in Section~\ref{sec:discussion} include single-run SRL ablations, confounds in the cross-dataset decomposition, and the exploratory nature of the cluster-split analysis.

Train/validation/test split files, feature-cluster split assignments, and evaluation scripts will be released alongside the source code upon acceptance.
